## Two factions in Cavite

By early 1897 the revolution against Spain was strongest in Cavite. But the Katipunan there was split between two local councils, the Magdiwang and the Magdalo. The Magdalo council was led by Emilio Aguinaldo, a young town official from Kawit who had won important early battles.[1][2]

Andrés Bonifacio, the Supremo who had founded the Katipunan in Manila, came to Cavite in December 1896 to help settle the conflict. He was an outsider there, from Tondo, without the land and family ties of the Cavite leaders.[1]

## Tejeros

On 22 March 1897, revolutionary leaders met at the estate house in Tejeros, in what is now General Trias. Bonifacio presided. The meeting decided to replace the Katipunan with a revolutionary government and to elect its officers.[2]

Aguinaldo, who was not present, was elected president. Bonifacio was elected Director of the Interior. Then a delegate, Daniel Tirona, objected that Bonifacio, who was not a lawyer, was not qualified for the post. Bonifacio, insulted, declared the results void and walked out.[1][2]

For Bonifacio, the Tejeros results were null. For Aguinaldo's side, the Katipunan had ceased to exist once the new government was elected.[2]

## Arrest

Bonifacio and his followers drew up documents rejecting the Tejeros government and began organizing on their own. Aguinaldo's side saw this as sedition. In late April 1897, Aguinaldo ordered Bonifacio's arrest, after reports that he was plotting against the revolutionary government.[2]

Soldiers found Bonifacio's group at Indang. A fight broke out. Bonifacio was wounded, his brother Ciriaco was killed, and his wife, Gregoria de Jesús, was mistreated.[1][2]

## The trial in Maragondon

Bonifacio and his brother Procopio were taken to Maragondon and tried by a council of war of the revolutionary government, in a house that still stands in the town. The trial took place in early May; sources give dates from 4 to 6 May. They were accused of treason and sedition.[3][4]

The trial was deeply unfair by any standard. Bonifacio's defense lawyer argued against him, and witnesses who might have helped him were not heard. On about 8 May, the court sentenced both brothers to death.[1][3]

## The commutation

What happened next is one of the most argued-over questions in Philippine history. Several accounts say that Aguinaldo first commuted the sentence to exile, then withdrew the commutation after generals, among them Mariano Noriel and Pío del Pilar, urged that Bonifacio was too dangerous to leave alive.[1][2] Others say Aguinaldo wanted to spare him but that his order came too late.[4]

## Mount Buntis

On 10 May 1897, soldiers under Major Lázaro Makapagal led Andrés and Procopio Bonifacio into the hills near Maragondon and shot them. They were buried in a shallow grave. Andrés was 33 years old.[2][3]

The exact place has been disputed. It is often given as Mount Buntis, in the area of Nagpatong. A monument stands in Maragondon near the traditional site.[2]

## The widow's story

Gregoria de Jesús, Bonifacio's widow, survived the attack at Indang and the trial. She was only 21 years old. In her later memoirs she described the attack at Indang, her husband's capture and the weeks that followed.[1]

Her account, along with the records of the trial, is one of the main sources historians use to understand what happened in those weeks of 1897. Because so many of the people involved were on Aguinaldo's side, her voice is especially important.[1][3]

## A wound in the nation's story

The death of Bonifacio is one of the most painful episodes in the country's history: the founder of the revolution, killed not by the enemy, but by his fellow revolutionaries.[1]

For generations it has fed debates about class, region and leadership in the revolution, and about how the history of 1897 should be told. Bonifacio is honored as a national hero, and 30 November, his birthday, is a national holiday. Aguinaldo went on to lead the revolution and to declare independence in 1898. Both men's names are still argued over.[1]
