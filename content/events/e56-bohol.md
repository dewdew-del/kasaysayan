## A holiday morning

Tuesday, 15 October 2013, was a national holiday in the Philippines, Eid al-Adha. Schools and most offices on Bohol were closed, and many families were at home or getting ready for the day.[4]

At 8:12 in the morning the ground began to shake. The earthquake, magnitude 7.2, was centered near the town of Sagbayan in the island's interior, at a shallow depth of about 12 kilometers. It lasted less than a minute, but it was felt across the Visayas, from Cebu to Leyte and Negros.[2][4]

Because it was a holiday, some people later said, the toll was lower than it might have been: many schools and government buildings that collapsed were empty.[4]

## Churches fall

Bohol is famous for its old stone churches, many built by Spanish missionaries and Filipino laborers in the 1700s and 1800s from blocks of coral stone. They were among the island's proudest landmarks and drew visitors from around the world.[1][2]

In minutes, many of them were cracked or brought down. The church of Our Lady of Light in Loon, one of the oldest and largest on the island, collapsed almost completely. Bell towers and façades fell in Loboc, Baclayon, Maribojoc and other towns.[1][3]

"If you see it, you would really cry," a bishop said after visiting the ruins.[1] For many Boholanos, losing the churches felt like losing part of their family history.

## Roads, bridges and homes

The damage went far beyond the churches. Roads cracked and split. Bridges collapsed, cutting towns off from each other. Landslides covered hillsides, and thousands of houses were damaged or destroyed.[4]

More than two hundred people were killed, with hundreds more injured. Tens of thousands of families were left without homes, many sleeping in tents and makeshift shelters for weeks.[2][4]

## A fault no one had mapped

Scientists from the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology, PHIVOLCS, went to find out what had caused the earthquake. They found it: a fault that had not been known before, running through the western part of Bohol. They named it the North Bohol Fault.[2]

In places, the ground along the fault had been pushed up by several meters, leaving a long, low wall of rock across rice fields and roads, like a step in the landscape.[2] Part of it has since been preserved as a site where visitors can see the fault's scar.

## Aftershocks and fear

For weeks after the main quake, thousands of aftershocks shook the island. Many families were too afraid to sleep indoors, even in houses that had survived, and camped in yards, plazas and fields.[4]

Volunteers, soldiers and aid groups brought food, water, tents and medicine. Engineers inspected schools and public buildings to decide which were safe to use. Roads and bridges were patched so that relief could reach the hardest-hit towns in the interior.[2][4]

## Then came Yolanda

Three weeks later, on 8 November, Typhoon Yolanda struck the Visayas, one of the most powerful storms ever recorded. Bohol was spared the worst of it, but the country's attention and aid were stretched even further, and families still living in tents had to shelter again.[4]

## Rebuilding the churches

The National Museum, the National Historical Commission and the Catholic Church began the slow work of restoring the damaged churches. Workers sorted through the rubble, numbering the fallen stones so that they could be put back where they belonged. Engineers studied how to strengthen the old walls against future quakes.[2][3]

The work took years. By 2020, the Diocese of Tagbilaran reported that 21 of 25 damaged churches in its area had been restored, and several in the Diocese of Talibon had also reopened.[3]

## What it changed

The earthquake changed how the country thinks about heritage buildings, earthquake safety and hidden faults beneath quiet towns. It also showed how much the people of Bohol valued their churches, not only as places of worship but as the living memory of their towns.[1][3]
