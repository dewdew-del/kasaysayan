## A movie star president

Joseph Estrada, a popular action-movie star known as "Erap," was elected president in 1998 by one of the largest margins in Philippine history. He was loved by many poor Filipinos who saw him as one of their own.[1]

Within two years his presidency was in crisis. In October 2000, a provincial governor, Luis "Chavit" Singson, accused Estrada of taking millions of pesos in payoffs from illegal gambling and tobacco taxes. Business groups, church leaders and many politicians called on him to resign.[1][2]

## The impeachment trial

In November 2000, the House of Representatives impeached Estrada. His trial before the Senate opened in December and was broadcast live on television, watched by millions.[1][2]

The prosecution tried to show that Estrada controlled secret bank accounts, including one under the name "Jose Velarde," worth hundreds of millions of pesos.[1]

## The second envelope

On 16 January 2001, the prosecution asked the court to open a sealed envelope containing bank records it said would link Estrada to the Velarde account. The senators acting as judges voted 11 to 10 not to open it.[1][2]

The prosecutors walked out in protest. Senate President Aquilino Pimentel resigned his post. Many viewers saw the vote as proof that Estrada's allies in the Senate would never convict him.[1]

{{photo:3}}

## Back to EDSA

That night, people began gathering at the EDSA Shrine in Ortigas, the site of the 1986 revolution. Text messages spread the call across Metro Manila: go to EDSA, wear black. By midnight, thousands had gathered.[1][2]

Over the next days, the crowd grew to hundreds of thousands. Students, workers, business people, priests and nuns rallied day and night, demanding that Estrada resign.[2][3]

## A text-message revolution

EDSA II was one of the first protests in the world organized largely through mobile phones. In 2001 the Philippines was already one of the world's heaviest users of text messaging, and messages calling people to EDSA, sharing news and jokes about the president spread faster than any newspaper.[1][3]

The crowds were different from 1986. Many were from the middle class and the business districts around Ortigas and Makati. Critics later pointed out that the poor, who had voted for Estrada in huge numbers, were less present.[1]

## The military withdraws

On 19 January, the chief of staff of the armed forces, General Angelo Reyes, and other military and police leaders went to EDSA and announced that they were withdrawing their support from Estrada. Cabinet members resigned one after another.[1][3]

## Arroyo sworn in

At about noon on 20 January 2001, Chief Justice Hilario Davide Jr. swore in Vice President Gloria Macapagal Arroyo as president at the EDSA Shrine. That afternoon, Estrada left Malacañang Palace by boat on the Pasig River. He said he was leaving for the sake of peace, but he did not formally resign.[1][3]

## A revolution argued over

Supporters called it EDSA II, a second people power revolution, this time against corruption. Critics, including many foreign journalists and some legal scholars, said it set a dangerous example: an elected president removed not by a finished trial or an election, but by crowds and the military.[3][4]

In March 2001, the Supreme Court ruled in Estrada v. Desierto that Estrada had effectively resigned, so Arroyo was the legitimate president.[4]

## What came after

In April 2001, Estrada was arrested on charges of plunder. His supporters, many of them poor, held their own large protest at EDSA, which ended in a violent march on Malacañang on 1 May, sometimes called "EDSA III."[1]

In 2007, a special court convicted Estrada of plunder. Weeks later, Arroyo pardoned him. He later ran for president again and was elected mayor of Manila. The events of January 2001 are still debated as an example of the strengths and dangers of people power, and of how hard it is to hold a powerful president to account through the courts alone.[1]
