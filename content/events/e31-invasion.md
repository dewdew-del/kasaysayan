## A Commonwealth getting ready

By 1941 the Philippines was halfway through its ten-year Commonwealth, preparing for full independence in 1946. Japan's empire was expanding across Asia. It had been at war in China since 1937 and had moved into French Indochina.[4]

The Commonwealth government had asked General Douglas MacArthur, a former US Army chief of staff, to build a Philippine army. In July 1941, with war looking likely, the United States brought the Philippine forces under American command as the US Army Forces in the Far East, or USAFFE, with MacArthur in charge.[4]

Many people in Manila believed the Americans could hold the islands. They were wrong.

{{photo:2}}

## Clark Field

Early on 8 December 1941, Philippine time, news arrived that Japan had attacked the American fleet at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii. Across the International Date Line it was still 7 December there.[3]

American commanders in the Philippines had several hours' warning. Yet around noon, when Japanese bombers and fighters from Formosa reached Clark Field in Pampanga, they found many American planes parked on the ground. In a single attack they destroyed much of the US air force in the Philippines.[3][4] Why the planes were caught on the ground has been argued over ever since.

## Bombs on Manila

In the days that followed, Japanese planes bombed airfields, the naval base at Cavite and the port of Manila. People in the city dug shelters, blacked out their windows and listened to the radio for news. Diaries from those days describe sirens, burning ships in the bay and the sudden fear of a city that had never been bombed.[2][3]

{{photo:3}}

## The landings

Small Japanese forces landed in northern Luzon within days. Then, on 22 December 1941, the main Japanese army, the 14th Army under Lieutenant General Masaharu Homma, landed in strength at Lingayen Gulf in Pangasinan. Two days later another force landed at Lamon Bay, southeast of Manila.[1]

Filipino and American troops, many of them poorly trained and equipped, could not stop them. MacArthur ordered a retreat to the Bataan Peninsula, west of Manila Bay, as an old war plan prescribed, hoping to hold out there until help arrived from the United States.[1][4]

## Manila, open city

To spare Manila from destruction, MacArthur declared it an open city on 26 December 1941. Military forces were withdrawn so that, under the rules of war, there would be no reason to attack it. Japanese planes bombed it anyway, including the old Santo Domingo Church in Intramuros.[2][4]

President Manuel Quezon, Vice President Sergio Osmeña and the government left Manila for the island fortress of Corregidor at the mouth of the bay.[4]

On 2 January 1942, Japanese troops marched into Manila.[4]

## The start of an occupation

The occupation that followed lasted more than three years. Japan set up a government of Filipino officials under its control, and in 1943 declared the Philippines an "independent" republic under President José P. Laurel, recognized by Japan and its allies but not by the Commonwealth government in exile or the United States.[4]

For ordinary Filipinos, the years that followed brought shortages of food, a currency that lost its value, the terror of the military police, and the growth of guerrilla resistance in the hills.[2] They also brought, in 1944 and 1945, a return of war to the islands that was even more destructive than its arrival.

## Why it matters

The fall of the Philippines was one of the greatest defeats in American military history, and for Filipinos it ended the sense that American protection was a guarantee. It also shaped the generation that would lead the country after independence: many of them had fought in Bataan, joined the guerrillas or worked under the occupation, and the choices people made in those years would be argued about for decades.[4]
