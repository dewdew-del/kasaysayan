## A sea with many claims

The South China Sea lies between mainland Southeast Asia, China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Borneo and Vietnam. It is one of the world's busiest shipping routes, rich in fish and possibly in oil and gas. The Philippines calls the part of it off its western coast the West Philippine Sea.[1][3]

Several countries claim parts of it, including the Philippines, China, Vietnam, Malaysia, Brunei and Taiwan. China's claim is the largest. On its maps, a "nine-dash line" loops far south from its coast, around most of the sea, close to the coasts of its neighbors.[2][3]

## Scarborough Shoal

Tensions rose sharply in 2012. At Scarborough Shoal, a ring of reefs about 220 kilometers west of Luzon where Filipino fishermen had long worked, Philippine and Chinese government ships faced off for weeks. Afterward, Chinese vessels stayed and kept Filipino fishermen away.[3][4]

China also began building artificial islands on reefs in the Spratly Islands, turning them into bases with airstrips and harbors.[2][3]

## Going to court

In January 2013, under President Benigno Aquino III, the Philippines took a step no country had taken against China before: it started an arbitration case under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, UNCLOS, which both countries had signed.[1][3]

China refused to take part. It said the tribunal had no jurisdiction over what it called questions of sovereignty. The case went ahead anyway, before a five-member tribunal, with the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague acting as its registry.[2][3]

## The award

On 12 July 2016, the tribunal released its award, about 500 pages long. It ruled, unanimously, largely in favor of the Philippines.[1][2]

Its key findings were that China's claim to "historic rights" to resources within the nine-dash line had no legal basis under the Convention. None of the features in the Spratly Islands was an island that could generate a 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone. China had violated the Philippines' rights by interfering with fishing and oil exploration, and by building artificial islands that caused severe harm to coral reefs.[2][3]

The tribunal did not decide who owned the islands and reefs themselves. That question was outside its powers.[2]

## Why it mattered beyond the two countries

The case mattered to more than the Philippines and China. Other countries with claims in the South China Sea, such as Vietnam and Malaysia, watched closely, because the tribunal's reasoning about historic rights and the status of reefs could apply to their own claims too.[2][3]

Many countries that use the sea for trade, including Japan, Australia, the United States and members of the European Union, called on both sides to respect the ruling, describing it as an important test of whether international law can settle disputes between large and small states.[3]

## China's answer

China rejected the award immediately, calling it "null and void" and saying it would not accept or recognize it. It continued its presence and patrols in the disputed waters, and kept building on the artificial islands it already held.[2][3]

## A victory, and what came after

For many Filipinos, the award was a landmark: a small country winning a legal case against a much larger one, based on international law. It is often called one of the most important legal victories in Philippine history.[1][4]

How to use it has divided Philippine leaders. Some presidents played the ruling down in favor of closer ties with China. Others have made it the foundation of the country's position, raising it at the United Nations and in talks with other countries.[1]

## The ruling today

Every 12 July, the government marks the anniversary.[1] The award has not stopped the confrontations at sea, where Philippine fishermen and coast guard ships still face Chinese vessels. But it gave the Philippines something it did not have before: a clear, binding international ruling to point to.[1][4]
