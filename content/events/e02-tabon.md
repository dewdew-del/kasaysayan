## A headland full of caves

On the west coast of Palawan, near the town of Quezon, a limestone headland called Lipuun Point rises above the sea. Its cliffs hold more than two hundred caves. Thirty-eight of them show signs that people lived or buried their dead there long ago. Together they are known as the Tabon Cave Complex.[2]

The largest, Tabon Cave, has a wide mouth high above the water. It is cool and dry, a good shelter from rain and sun. For thousands of years people came back to it, leaving behind stone flakes, animal bones, ash from cooking fires and, later, pottery.[2]

## The dig

In 1962, the American anthropologist Robert B. Fox of the National Museum of the Philippines began excavating Tabon Cave with a Filipino team. Digging through the layers of the cave floor was like reading a calendar backwards: the deeper they went, the older the finds.[1]

On 28 May 1962 they recovered part of a human skull, the top of the head or skullcap. It became famous as "Tabon Man," although later study suggests it may have belonged to a woman. For decades it was described as the oldest human fossil in the country.[1]

## What the floor remembered

The layers held more than bones. Fox's team recovered thousands of stone flakes, many made from chert, a hard stone that breaks into sharp edges. People had struck them off larger cores to cut meat, scrape hides and shape wood. The tools changed very little over thousands of years, which suggests the cave was used again and again by people with a long-lasting way of life.[1]

Charcoal from ancient hearths, the remains of animals they ate and the position of each find in the soil helped the team build a sequence of occupation, from the Ice Age down to much more recent times.[1]

## Two bones, two dates

Getting the age right has taken a long time. Early estimates put the skullcap at around 22,000 years old. In the early 2000s, a National Museum team led by Eusebio Dizon re-dated it by testing the bone itself with the uranium-series method. The result was about 16,500 years, give or take 2,000.[1]

The re-excavation that started in 2000 also turned up a fragment of a human shin bone, or tibia, from a deeper layer. That fragment was dated to about 47,000 years ago, with a wide margin of error.[1] This is where the big number often quoted for "Tabon Man" really comes from, and why this page marks the dates as contested: popular accounts often give the skullcap the shin bone's age.

Either way, Tabon showed that modern humans lived on Palawan during the Ice Age, when lower seas made the island far easier to reach from the rest of Southeast Asia. In 2010, a foot bone from Callao Cave in Cagayan was dated to about 67,000 years, taking the title of oldest human remains in the country from Tabon.[3]

## Jars for the dead

The Tabon caves were used for burial long after the Ice Age ended. In nearby Manunggul Cave, Fox's team found a large earthenware burial jar made around 3,000 years ago. It is a secondary burial jar: the bones of the dead were placed inside it some time after the first burial.[2]

On its lid sit two figures in a boat. They are usually read as a soul being ferried to the afterlife by a guide, an idea that still echoes in boat-shaped burials elsewhere in the islands. The jar was declared a National Cultural Treasure and is one of the best-loved objects at the National Museum of Anthropology in Manila. It also appeared on the old thousand-peso bill.[2]

## A protected place

The Tabon Cave Complex itself was declared a National Cultural Treasure by the National Museum in 2011, and it is on the Philippines' tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage status.[2] A site museum opened at Lipuun Point in 2024.[2]

Visitors who climb the steps to the mouth of Tabon Cave can look out at the same sea that the first islanders saw. The cave floor they stand on still holds layers of a story that archaeologists are not finished reading.
