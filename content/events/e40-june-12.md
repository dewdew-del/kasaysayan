## Two independence days

For sixteen years after 1946, the Philippines celebrated its independence on 4 July, the day the United States had formally recognized the Republic at the Luneta. It was also the American Independence Day. Each year the two countries' flags flew together, and the date was a reminder of how closely the Philippines was tied to its former ruler.[2][3]

But there was another date. On 12 June 1898, in Kawit, Cavite, Emilio Aguinaldo's revolutionary government had declared the Philippines independent of Spain. That declaration had never been recognized by Spain or the United States, but to many Filipinos it was the true birth of the nation.[2]

## A president's decision

Diosdado Macapagal, elected president in 1961, was a lawyer from Lubao, Pampanga, who had risen from a poor family and who spoke often about national identity and pride. On 12 May 1962 he signed Proclamation No. 28, declaring 12 June a special public holiday as Independence Day.[1][3]

The proclamation explained its reasoning: on 12 June 1898, the revolutionary government had declared independence and established a Philippine republic, and that was the day Filipinos had won their freedom by their own efforts.[1]

That year the first 12 June celebration was held, with Aguinaldo himself, then 93 years old, as a guest of honor.[2]

## A quarrel with Washington

The timing had another side. In early 1962, the US Congress had rejected a bill to pay $73 million in war damage claims that Filipinos had been waiting on since the end of the Second World War. Many Filipinos were angry.[2][3]

Some historians see the move to 12 June partly as a protest: a way of telling the United States, politely, that Philippine independence was not an American gift. Macapagal himself said it was mainly about national pride.[2]

## The Kawit connection

Moving the holiday also changed where the country looked on its biggest day. Instead of the Luneta, where the American flag had come down in 1946, attention turned to Kawit, to the house where the declaration had been read, and to the revolution against Spain.[2]

For the old veterans of the revolution who were still alive in 1962, it was a moment of recognition. Their struggle, which had ended in defeat by the Americans, was now officially remembered as the founding of the nation.[2][3]

## Made into law

Macapagal's proclamation was confirmed by Congress two years later. Republic Act No. 4166, signed on 4 August 1964, made 12 June the official Philippine Independence Day and renamed 4 July as Philippine Republic Day.[3][4]

Today 4 July is observed as Philippine–American Friendship Day, a working day, while 12 June is the country's most important national holiday.[3]

## What independence means

The change sparked a debate that still continues: which date really marks independence?[3]

Those who favor 12 June say it honors the Filipinos who fought and died for freedom, and that a country's independence begins when its people claim it. Those who point to 4 July say that in 1898 the Philippines was not actually free: within months it was at war with the United States and lost. Independence, they argue, became real only in 1946, when it was recognized internationally.[2][3]

The historian Ambeth Ocampo has summed up the story in three words: independence was "declared, taken away, given back."[2] Both dates are part of that story.

## Celebrating 12 June

Every 12 June, the president leads ceremonies, often at the Aguinaldo Shrine in Kawit or in Manila, and towns across the country hold flag-raisings and parades. The Philippine flag, sewn in Hong Kong in 1898, is flown everywhere.[3]

The decision of 1962 changed how millions of Filipinos understand the beginning of their nation: not with a flag being lowered by foreigners, but with a flag being raised by Filipinos themselves.[2]
