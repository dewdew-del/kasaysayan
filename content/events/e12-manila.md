## A walled town by the river

Before the Spanish came, a settlement called Maynila stood on the south bank of the Pasig River where it flows into Manila Bay. It was ruled by Rajah Sulayman, together with an older relative, Rajah Matanda. Across the river was Tondo, ruled by Lakan Dula. Both were trading towns, linked to Brunei, China and the wider Muslim and Malay world.[1]

Maynila was protected by a wooden stockade and armed with cannon. Its people traded rice, gold and other goods with Chinese and Bornean merchants. To the Spanish in the Visayas, it looked like the richest prize in the islands.[1][3]

## The first clash

In 1570, Legazpi sent his field commander, Martín de Goiti, with a force of Spaniards and Visayan allies to Manila Bay. Talks with Sulayman broke down, fighting followed, and the town was set on fire. Goiti withdrew, but the Spanish had seen Maynila's wealth and position.[1]

In 1571 Legazpi himself came north with a larger fleet. This time the rulers of Maynila and Tondo, facing a stronger force, accepted terms, and the Spanish took over the site.[1][3]

## A city and a council

On 19 May 1571, Legazpi took formal possession of Maynila. On 24 June, he set up a city council, the cabildo, with Spanish officials, and declared Manila the capital of the Spanish colony in the islands.[2][3] The king of Spain later confirmed its title as a city and granted it a coat of arms.[2]

Manila's position made it ideal. Its bay was wide and sheltered, the Pasig led inland to the lake and rich farmland, and it faced the trade routes to China. From here the Spanish would rule the islands for more than three centuries.[1]

## Bangkusay

Not everyone accepted the new order. In early June 1571, a fleet of warriors from towns around the bay, led by a chief from Macabebe in Pampanga, sailed against the Spanish. On 3 June they met Spanish ships in the Bangkusay Channel off Tondo.[1]

The Spanish, with better guns and armor, won the battle, and the Macabebe chief was killed. Some accounts say he was Rajah Sulayman, but Spanish records suggest Sulayman survived, and historians still debate who the fallen leader was.[1][3] His name is remembered today as one of the first Filipinos to die resisting Spanish rule.

## The Chinese of the Parián

Manila's growth depended on Chinese traders and craftsmen. Every year junks came from Fujian with goods for the galleons, and many Chinese stayed to work as merchants, bakers, tailors, carpenters and stonemasons. The Spanish settled them in a district outside the walls called the Parián.[1]

The relationship was uneasy. The Spanish needed the Chinese for trade and labor but feared their numbers, and tensions broke out more than once in violence. Over time, many Chinese married Filipino women and became Catholic, and their descendants, the Chinese mestizos, became an important part of Philippine society.[1]

## Building Intramuros

Over the following decades, the Spanish replaced the old stockade with a city of stone. Walls rose around it, giving it the name Intramuros, "within the walls." Inside were the governor's palace, the cathedral, churches and convents of the religious orders, schools, and the houses of Spanish families.[1][2]

The walls were not only for defense against foreign attack. They also marked a division. Filipinos and Chinese mostly lived outside them, in towns such as Tondo, Binondo and Quiapo, which became busy centers of trade and craft.[1]

## A capital for centuries

Manila remained the capital through the Spanish and American periods and into independence. Much of Intramuros was destroyed in the Battle of Manila in 1945, but parts of the walls, Fort Santiago and San Agustin Church survived, and the area has been restored as a historic district.[1][2]

When people celebrate Manila Day each June, they remember the founding of the Spanish city in 1571. Historians add a reminder: Maynila was already an old and important town long before Legazpi arrived.[1]
