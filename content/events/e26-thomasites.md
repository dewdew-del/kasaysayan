## Schools as a weapon of peace

While the Philippine–American War was still being fought, the American colonial government decided that schools would be one of its most important tools. Education, officials argued, would show Filipinos the benefits of American rule and prepare them, eventually, for self-government.[2]

In January 1901, the Philippine Commission passed Act No. 74, which created a Department of Public Instruction and a system of free primary schools across the islands. English would be the language of instruction.[1][2]

There was one problem: there were very few teachers who could teach in English. The government decided to bring them from the United States.

{{photo:2}}

## The voyage of the Thomas

On 23 July 1901, the US Army transport ship Thomas left San Francisco carrying hundreds of young American teachers, men and women, many of them recent college graduates. Sources give different totals, but the group was usually counted at around 500.[1][2]

After a stop in Honolulu, the Thomas reached Manila Bay on 21 August 1901. Because of a cholera outbreak, the teachers were held on board for a couple of days before they could land.[1][2] Smaller groups of teachers had arrived on earlier ships, but the Thomas carried the largest, and soon all the pioneer American teachers came to be called Thomasites.[2]

## Spread across the islands

The teachers were sent all over the archipelago, from big provincial towns to remote villages in the mountains and on small islands. Many lived far from other Americans, learning local languages and adjusting to unfamiliar food, climate and customs.[2][3]

Life was hard. Some caught tropical diseases. Some were killed in the ongoing fighting. Others gave up and went home. But many stayed for years, building schools and training Filipino teachers who would gradually take over.[2]

{{photo:3}}

## English, everywhere

The Thomasites' most lasting legacy was the English language. Within a few decades, English became the language of government, law, business and higher education in the Philippines. It is why the Philippines today has one of the largest English-speaking populations in Asia.[4]

They also helped build institutions. The Philippine Normal School, founded in 1901 to train teachers, became the Philippine Normal University. Trade schools, agricultural schools and the University of the Philippines, founded in 1908, grew out of the same push to build a public education system.[2]

## Filipino teachers take over

The Thomasites were never meant to stay forever. From the start, part of their job was to train Filipinos to teach. Within about two decades, most public school teachers in the country were Filipino, many of them women for whom teaching was one of the first professions open to them.[2]

Schools also spread fast. Enrollment in public schools grew from tens of thousands in the early 1900s to more than a million by the 1920s.[2] For the first time, children in small towns could go to school without paying.

## Two ways to remember them

The Thomasites are remembered in two ways. For many Filipino families, public schools opened the door to literacy and new jobs for the first time, and some teachers are still recalled with affection in the towns where they served.[3][4]

At the same time, historians point out that the schools were part of a colonial project. Lessons taught American history, values and heroes, often more than Filipino ones, and using English pushed aside Filipino languages in the classroom. Some critics have described this as a "miseducation" that taught Filipinos to see their own culture as inferior.[2][4]

## A long shadow

The questions the Thomasites raised are still alive: which language children should learn in, what history they should be taught, and how a country can keep its own identity while learning from others.[4]

In 1961, the United States launched the Peace Corps, sending young volunteers abroad as teachers and helpers. One of the first countries to receive them was the Philippines, and the volunteers were often compared to the Thomasites of sixty years earlier.[4]
