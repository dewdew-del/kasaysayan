## Eight years of martial law

By the start of 1981, the Philippines had been under martial law for more than eight years. President Ferdinand Marcos governed by decree. Congress had been closed since 1972, though an Interim Batasang Pambansa elected in 1978 now met. Thousands of people had been detained, and many critics were in exile.[2][4]

The economy, which had grown in the early 1970s, was in trouble. Foreign debt was rising fast. Prices of the country's main exports, sugar and coconut, had fallen. The government faced an armed communist insurgency in many provinces and a Muslim rebellion in Mindanao.[2]

Pressure was building, both inside the country and abroad.

## A pope is coming

In early 1981, Pope John Paul II was scheduled to visit the Philippines in February, the first papal visit to the country since Paul VI in 1970. The Philippines was the largest Catholic nation in Asia, and the visit would bring the world's media to Manila.[1][3]

Church leaders, especially Cardinal Jaime Sin, had grown increasingly critical of martial law. The government did not want the pope arriving in a country under military rule.[3]

## Proclamation 2045

On 17 January 1981, Marcos signed Proclamation No. 2045, formally lifting martial law throughout the country, except in two regions of Mindanao where the government was fighting Muslim separatists. He announced the birth of a "New Republic."[1][2]

Political prisoners were released in larger numbers, and some restrictions on travel and the press were eased.[2]

{{photo:3}}

## Powers that stayed

Critics quickly pointed out that very little had really changed. The decrees issued under martial law stayed in force. Under Amendment No. 6 to the 1973 Constitution, added in 1976, Marcos could still issue decrees with the force of law whenever he judged there was a "grave emergency." He also kept the power to order arrests of people he considered threats to national security.[1][2]

One opposition leader said the country still had "a government of one man" whose powers no law could limit.[2] Many Filipinos called it a cosmetic change, made for the cameras.

## The pope's visit

Pope John Paul II arrived on 17 February 1981. At Malacañang Palace, with Marcos beside him, he said that even in exceptional situations, a government could never justify violating the basic dignity of the human person.[3] His words were understood by many as a criticism of the regime.

During the trip he also beatified Lorenzo Ruiz, a Filipino martyr killed in Japan in the 1600s, in a ceremony in Manila.[3]

## Why lift it at all

Historians point to several reasons for the timing. The papal visit was one. Another was the United States, where a new president, Ronald Reagan, was about to take office and where Congress and the press had been questioning American support for a military regime.[1][3]

There were also reasons at home. Foreign banks and investors, whose loans the Philippine economy depended on, wanted to see stability and a return to normal government. Opposition leaders, students and church groups were growing bolder. Ending martial law on paper, while keeping real power, was a way to answer all of them at once.[1][2]

## A new election

In June 1981, Marcos held a presidential election under the amended constitution. Most of the opposition boycotted it, calling it a sham. Marcos won easily against a little-known candidate and was sworn in for a new six-year term.[1][2]

## What changed and what did not

For ordinary people, the lifting of martial law brought some relief: fewer curfews and somewhat more open newspapers and campus protests. But arrests, torture and killings of activists continued, especially in the countryside.[2][4]

The real end of Marcos's power came five years later, in the People Power Revolution of February 1986. Historians often describe the period from 1981 to 1986 as martial law in all but name, a dictatorship with a new label.[1][4]
