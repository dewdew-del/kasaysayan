## The sugar island

Negros is the sugar island of the Philippines. For more than a century, its plains were divided into great haciendas owned by a few families, worked by tens of thousands of sacadas and farmworkers who cut cane for low wages.[1][4]

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the price of sugar on the world market collapsed. The government's sugar monopoly, run by a close ally of President Marcos, took much of the profit that remained. Haciendas cut jobs and wages. By 1985, Negros was suffering a famine. Photographs of starving children from the island were seen around the world.[1][3]

## A strike for the people

In September 1985, workers' groups, farmers' organizations and church groups in northern Negros called a welgang bayan, a "people's strike," to protest hunger, low wages and the abuses of martial law. They timed it around the anniversary of the declaration of martial law on 21 September.[1][4]

Thousands of people, mostly hacienda workers from towns across northern Negros, gathered in Escalante, a sugar town on the island's northeastern coast. They set up a picket in front of the town hall.[1]

## The dispersal

On the afternoon of 20 September 1985, soldiers, police and members of the Civilian Home Defense Forces, a paramilitary group, moved to break up the protest. They used tear gas and water cannons. Then they opened fire on the crowd.[1][4]

According to the Bantayog ng mga Bayani, which documented the event, the gunfire came from the government forces. The protesters were unarmed. People ran, fell and tried to hide behind vehicles and walls.[1]

## Twenty dead

Twenty people were killed. Most were young sugarcane workers and farmers. Some were teenagers. About thirty others were wounded.[1][4]

The Bantayog ng mga Bayani, the memorial to the victims of martial law in Quezon City, lists each of the twenty dead by name, with their stories: young workers, organizers and students who had come to Escalante to ask for food and justice.[1]

## The victims

The dead were not famous. They included young sacadas who had never been to school, farmers' organizers, a few student activists, and young women from the haciendas around Escalante. Some had walked for hours to join the protest.[1]

Their families, many of them landless, could not afford lawyers. In the years that followed, human rights groups and church workers helped them record what had happened and keep the memory alive.[2][4]

After 1986, some of the victims' names were added to the Wall of Remembrance at the Bantayog ng mga Bayani in Quezon City, alongside other victims and heroes of the martial law years.[1]

## No one held responsible

The government said soldiers had been attacked first, a claim that survivors denied. No soldier, police officer or militiaman was ever convicted for the killings.[3]

The massacre added to the anger that was sweeping the country after the assassination of Ninoy Aquino two years before. Less than five months later, the Marcos government fell in the People Power Revolution.[2][4]

## Remembered on Negros

Every September, people in Escalante and across Negros gather to remember the massacre. Church groups, farmworkers' unions and families of the victims hold Masses, marches and cultural programs.[2]

For the people of Negros, Escalante is a reminder that the martial law years were felt most harshly not only in Manila but in the countryside, among the poorest workers, who had the least protection.[2][3]

## The land question

The deeper problem behind the protest, land, did not disappear after 1986. The Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Program of 1988 was supposed to give farmworkers land of their own. In Negros, its progress was slow and often resisted.[3] Many of the families who marched in Escalante in 1985 are still farmworkers today, working land that belongs to someone else, as their parents did.
