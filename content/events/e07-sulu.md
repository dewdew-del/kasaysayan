## Islands between two worlds

The Sulu Archipelago is a chain of hundreds of islands strung between the tip of Mindanao and the coast of Borneo. Its people, the Tausug and their neighbors, were sailors, divers and traders. For centuries their waters were a crossroads where goods from China, the spice islands and the rest of Southeast Asia changed hands.[2]

The largest town, Jolo, sits on a sheltered bay on the island of the same name. Ships could anchor there safely, and the town grew rich on trade in pearls, mother-of-pearl, sea cucumbers and other sea products that were in demand in China.[2]

## Islam arrives

Islam came to Sulu gradually, carried by traders and teachers from across the Muslim world of Southeast Asia. Sulu tradition remembers several early figures. One, Tuan Masha'ika, is said to have settled in Jolo much earlier. Another, a missionary named Karim ul-Makhdum, is said to have arrived in the late 1300s and built a mosque on the island of Simunul, in Tawi-Tawi.[1][2]

These accounts come from the tarsila, family genealogies kept by Sulu's ruling houses. They are an important source, but they were written down long after the events they describe, and historians use them with care.[1]

## The first sultan

According to the tarsila, a scholar from Arabia by way of the Malay world, known as Sharif ul-Hashim, or Sayyid Abu Bakr, came to Sulu in the 1400s. He married into a local ruling family, taught Islamic law and was recognized as the first sultan.[1][3]

As sultan he is credited with organizing the state along Islamic lines: a council of leaders, laws based on Islamic teaching mixed with local custom, and a system of officials and local datus who owed loyalty to the sultan.[2]

When this happened is one of the open questions of Philippine history. Some sources give a very exact date, 17 November 1405. Many modern historians prefer 1457. Others simply say "around 1450."[2] This site uses the 1400s and marks the date as contested.

## A state built on the sea

The sultanate's wealth came from the sea. Divers gathered pearls and mother-of-pearl from the reefs. Traders bought sea cucumbers, birds' nests and shells from across the archipelago and sold them to Chinese merchants, who came to Jolo every year. In return Sulu received porcelain, silk, iron and, later, firearms.[2]

Control of that trade, and of the boats and men who carried it, was the basis of the sultan's power. Local datus led their own followers and fleets, and the sultan's authority depended on keeping their loyalty through marriage, gifts, law and religion.[2] It was a state that looked outward to the sea rather than inward to farmland, and it was very good at it.

## A sea power

Over the next centuries the sultanate grew into one of the strongest states in the region. At times it controlled not only the Sulu islands but parts of Mindanao and northeastern Borneo, where the sultan of Brunei is said to have granted Sulu rights to territory in what is now Sabah.[2]

Sulu fought the Spanish for more than three hundred years. Spanish forces attacked Jolo many times, but the sultanate kept its independence for most of the colonial period, trading with the British, the Dutch and the Chinese.[2][3]

## Long decline, long memory

Under American rule, the sultan's political powers were reduced step by step. In 1915 the sultan of the time, Jamalul Kiram II, signed an agreement giving up temporal power to the American government while keeping his religious role.[2]

Questions from that history, including the old claim to Sabah, still come up in Philippine and Malaysian politics today.[3] For the Tausug and other Muslim Filipinos, the sultanate remains a source of pride: proof that a strong, organized state existed in the southern Philippines long before the Spanish arrived, and that it was never fully conquered.
