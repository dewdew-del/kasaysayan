## From resistance to rebellion

After the Second World War, the Hukbalahap guerrillas of Central Luzon expected to be honored for fighting the Japanese. Instead, many of their leaders were arrested, landlords returned with private armies, and Luis Taruc and other Huk-backed congressmen elected in 1946 were not allowed to take their seats.[1][4]

The Huks went back to the hills and took up arms against the government of the new Republic. By 1950 they had thousands of fighters and controlled large areas of Central Luzon. Some government officials feared they might even march on Manila.[4]

{{photo:2}}

## Magsaysay's campaign

The man who turned the tide was Ramon Magsaysay, a former guerrilla leader from Zambales whom President Elpidio Quirino made secretary of defense in 1950. Magsaysay reorganized the army, removed corrupt officers and told soldiers to treat villagers with respect, so that farmers would stop helping the rebels.[1][4]

He also offered rebels a way out. Those who surrendered could receive land in resettlement areas in Mindanao, run by the army's Economic Development Corps. The government arrested much of the Communist Party's leadership in Manila in 1950, a blow from which the movement never fully recovered.[4]

In 1953 Magsaysay was elected president by a landslide.

## A journalist in the hills

By early 1954 the Huks were weakened, divided and tired. Taruc himself had broken with the Communist Party's leaders over how to continue the struggle.[1][3]

President Magsaysay sent emissaries to talk to him. One was a 21-year-old reporter for the Manila Times, Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr., the son of a prominent Tarlac politician. Aquino first met Taruc in hiding on 10 February 1954 and returned several times for secret talks.[1][3]

The meetings were dangerous. Aquino traveled through rebel territory to reach Taruc's hideouts, and either side could have turned on him.

{{photo:3}}

## The surrender

On 17 May 1954, after four months of negotiation, Taruc came out of hiding and surrendered. Time magazine reported the event as the surrender of the most famous communist leader in Asia, arranged with the approval of President Magsaysay.[2] Accounts differ on the exact form of the surrender, including whether Taruc gave himself up to Magsaysay personally.[1][3]

Taruc was tried, pleaded guilty to rebellion and was sentenced to prison. He served about 14 years before he was pardoned in 1968.[1] He later said he had expected an earlier pardon, and blamed Aquino for not winning it.[3]

## The end of the Huks

Taruc's surrender is generally seen as the end of the Huk rebellion as a major threat.[4] Smaller groups kept fighting for years, and some Huk remnants turned to crime. Most former fighters returned to their villages.

The deeper problems that had fed the rebellion, landlessness and poverty in the countryside, were only partly addressed. Land reform laws were passed but often weakly enforced.[1]

## Two men's later lives

The surrender shaped the lives of both men. It made Ninoy Aquino famous at a young age. He went on to become the country's youngest governor and senator, the leading opponent of Ferdinand Marcos, and a martyr in 1983.[1]

Taruc, after his release, worked for land reform and peasant causes, and served briefly as a congressman. He died in 2005. Opinions about him remain divided: a hero of the resistance against Japan to some, a communist rebel to others, and to many simply a peasant leader who fought for the poor.[1]

## A lesson studied abroad

Magsaysay's campaign against the Huks was advised by Americans, including the Air Force officer Edward Lansdale, who became famous for his ideas on fighting rebellions by winning hearts and minds.[4]


The defeat of the Huks became a model studied by military officers in other countries fighting rebellions, including the United States in Vietnam. Its lesson, that winning the support of villagers mattered as much as winning battles, was easier to state than to follow.[4]
