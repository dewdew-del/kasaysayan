## Two armies around Manila

By early 1899, two armies faced each other around Manila. Inside the city were American troops, sent after the victory over Spain in Manila Bay. Around it, in an arc of trenches and blockhouses, were soldiers of the Philippine Republic, inaugurated at Malolos on 23 January.[1]

The two had fought on the same side against Spain only months before. But the United States had bought the islands from Spain under the Treaty of Paris, signed in December 1898, and President William McKinley had announced that America would govern them. Filipinos who had just declared independence refused to accept a new colonial master.[1][2]

For weeks, tension built along the lines. Patrols from both sides moved close to each other in the dark. Everyone expected that something would happen.[1]

## A shot in Santa Mesa

On the night of 4 February 1899, an American patrol of the 1st Nebraska Volunteers was walking near the San Juan River in Santa Mesa, on the eastern edge of Manila. According to the American account, Private William Walter Grayson saw a group of Filipino soldiers, called out "Halt," and when they did not stop, fired.[1][2]

Shooting spread along the lines within minutes. By the next day, the Battle of Manila was being fought across the eastern edge of the city. American troops, backed by artillery and gunboats on the Pasig River, drove the Filipinos back with heavy losses.[2]

Historians point out that the shots of 4 February only set off a conflict that had been building for months. Each side blamed the other for starting it.[1]

## A war of two kinds

At first, the Filipino army fought a conventional war, trying to hold lines and towns. Against better-armed and better-supplied American troops, it lost battle after battle. Malolos fell in March 1899.[2]

Late in 1899, the Filipino leadership changed strategy and turned to guerrilla warfare. Small units fought from the countryside, supported by villagers, ambushing American patrols and then melting away. The war spread across Luzon and into the Visayas and parts of Mindanao.[1][3]

## The cost to civilians

The Americans answered with harsh measures. In some provinces, villagers were forced into "protected zones," sometimes compared to concentration camps, so that the guerrillas would have no support. Crops, livestock and houses outside the zones were destroyed. Torture of prisoners, including the "water cure," was reported and investigated by the US Senate.[1][3]

The suffering was enormous. War, famine and disease, especially cholera, killed huge numbers of Filipinos. Most historians estimate at least 200,000 civilian deaths, and some estimates are much higher. Thousands of Filipino soldiers and more than four thousand American soldiers also died, though sources give different figures.[1][2]

## Filipinos on both sides

The war divided Filipinos too. Some wealthy and educated Filipinos decided early that the Americans would win and began working with them, joining the new colonial government or the Federal Party, which supported American rule.[1]

Others fought to the end. Generals such as Antonio Luna, Gregorio del Pilar, Miguel Malvar and Vicente Lukbán led Filipino forces in different parts of the country. Luna was killed in 1899 by soldiers of the Republic itself, in another bitter episode of rivalry within the revolution.[2]

## The official end

Emilio Aguinaldo was captured in Palanan, Isabela, in March 1901. In July 1902, President Theodore Roosevelt declared the "Philippine Insurrection" over.[1] Fighting continued for years afterward in parts of the country, including Batangas, Samar and the Muslim south.

## What to call it

For decades, American textbooks called it the "Philippine Insurrection," as if Filipinos had rebelled against a lawful government. In 1999, the US Library of Congress changed its subject heading to "Philippine–American War," the name Filipino historians had long used.[1]

The name matters. It recognizes that this was a war between two countries, one of which had just declared its independence. In the Philippines, the war's sites are remembered by markers and by groups who retrace its battlefields, including the bridge on the San Juan River where it began.[3]
