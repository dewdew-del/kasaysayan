## A city between two rivers

Ormoc is a port city on the western coast of Leyte, built on a plain where two rivers, the Anilao and the Malbasag, flow down from the mountains into Ormoc Bay. In 1991 it was a busy town of sugar fields, markets and fishing boats, with many families living along the riverbanks.[1][2]

The hills above the city had once been covered in forest. By the 1990s, much of that forest had been cut down for timber, fuel and farmland.[1]

## A weak storm, a lot of rain

In early November 1991, a tropical storm crossed the central Philippines. Its international name was Thelma; in the Philippines it was called Uring. It was not a powerful storm by wind speed. What made it dangerous was rain.[4]

On the morning of 5 November, the storm stalled over Leyte. For hours, heavy rain poured onto the hills above Ormoc. The bare slopes could not hold it. Water, mud, rocks and fallen logs poured into the Anilao and Malbasag watershed.[1][4]

## The flood

At about mid-morning, a flash flood swept down both rivers into the city. It came with almost no warning, in a matter of minutes. A wall of brown water, carrying uprooted trees and debris, swept through the low-lying neighborhoods along the rivers.[1][2]

The water rose to about three meters in parts of the city. Houses were torn from their foundations and carried into the bay. A high tide made things worse, pushing the water back into the town.[3] People climbed onto roofs, clung to trees or were swept away.

## The toll

When the water went down, Ormoc was in ruins. Bodies lay in the streets, in the mud and in the bay. The government struggled to count the dead. Early reports gave about 3,000. Later counts rose above 5,000, with some estimates reaching 8,000 when the missing were included.[3][4]

Many victims were never found. Some were buried in mass graves because there were too many to identify. It remains one of the deadliest weather disasters in Philippine history, with most of its victims from Ormoc itself.[1][4]

## Stories of survival

Survivors later told of climbing onto rooftops as their neighborhoods disappeared under water, of being swept into the bay and pulled out by fishermen, and of losing whole families in minutes.[2]

In some households, only one or two members survived. Many children were left orphans. The disaster touched almost every family in the city.[1][2]

## Rescue and relief

Relief efforts were slowed by more rain, damaged roads and the sheer scale of the disaster. Soldiers, volunteers and aid groups worked to rescue survivors, provide food and clean water, and prevent disease.[3]

The disaster came only five months after the eruption of Mount Pinatubo, when the country's emergency services were already stretched thin.[4]

## Why it happened

Investigators and environmentalists pointed to deforestation in the watershed above the city as a major reason the flood was so sudden and so deadly. Without trees to hold the soil and slow the water, the rain ran straight off the hills and carried logs and debris with it.[1][2]

The tragedy led to new attention to forest protection and watershed management, and to programs to replant the hills above Ormoc. Logging in the area came under closer control, and the disaster became a lesson taught in schools about how healthy forests protect the towns below them.[1][2]

## Remembered every November

Every 5 November, Ormoc remembers. Families gather at memorials and mass graves, light candles and lay flowers for relatives lost in the flood.[2]

The city has rebuilt, and the river channels were widened and lined to carry floodwaters. But the memory of 1991 is still a reminder of how deadly rain can be when the land that should hold it has been stripped bare.[1][2]
