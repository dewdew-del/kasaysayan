## A government in the making

After declaring independence in June 1898, Emilio Aguinaldo's government needed to turn a revolution into a state. His adviser Apolinario Mabini, a lawyer from Batangas paralyzed by polio, drafted much of the plan for how the new government would work.[2]

Aguinaldo called a congress of representatives from the provinces. Some were elected, others appointed where fighting made elections impossible. They were mostly educated, wealthy men: lawyers, doctors, landowners and merchants.[2]

## A congress in a church

The congress opened on 15 September 1898 in the Barasoain Church in Malolos, a town in Bulacan north of Manila, chosen because it was safer than the capital, where American troops were stationed.[2]

Its most important task was writing a constitution. Debates were long and sometimes bitter. One of the hardest questions was religion: whether the new republic would keep Catholicism as a state religion or separate church and state. In the end, by a narrow vote, the congress chose separation, a bold choice for a mostly Catholic country.[2][4]

## The Malolos Constitution

The constitution set up a republic with three branches of government: an executive led by a president, a single-chamber assembly of representatives, and courts. It included a bill of rights protecting freedom of speech, religion and association.[1][2]

The congress approved the document in January 1899, and it was promulgated on 21 January. It was the first democratic constitution in Asia.[1]

## Who could vote

The Malolos Constitution was democratic for its time, but it was not democratic in the way we use the word today. Voting for the first assembly was limited mostly to educated and property-owning men, and women could not vote at all.[2]

Historians still debate how much the Republic represented ordinary Filipinos. Its leaders were largely the ilustrado elite, and some of the peasants and workers who had fought in the Katipunan felt left out.[2] Mabini himself argued for a strong executive during wartime and clashed with the conservative members of congress.

## Inauguration day

Two days later, on 23 January 1899, the Philippine Republic was inaugurated at Barasoain. Aguinaldo took his oath as president. In his inaugural speech, he spoke of the sacrifices of the revolution and the hopes of the new nation, and he asked other countries to recognize it.[3][4]

Crowds filled the church and the plaza outside. There were parades, music and fireworks. To the people of Malolos, it seemed that the long struggle had ended in a free country.[4]

## A republic at war

The celebration did not last. The United States, which had bought the islands from Spain in the Treaty of Paris, refused to recognize the Republic. On 4 February 1899, less than two weeks after the inauguration, fighting broke out between Filipino and American troops in Manila.[1][3]

The Republic's government moved from town to town as American forces advanced. In March 1899, Malolos itself fell. Aguinaldo retreated north into the mountains, and his government became a government on the run.[1]

## How long did it last?

Aguinaldo was captured in Palanan, Isabela, in March 1901, and soon after he swore allegiance to the United States. Historians usually date the end of the First Republic to that year.[1]

It lasted only about two years and controlled only part of the islands. It was never recognized by any foreign country. But it was a real government with a written constitution, a congress and an army, and it proved that Filipinos could build their own state.[1][2]

## Remembered at Barasoain

In 2013, a presidential proclamation declared 23 January as First Philippine Republic Day, a working holiday to remember the event.[1] Barasoain Church, often called the "Cradle of Democracy in the East," still stands in Malolos. Its image appeared on the old ten-peso bill, and every January the province of Bulacan holds ceremonies there to mark the Republic's founding.[4]
