## A government on the run

By late 1899, the Philippine–American War was going badly for the Republic. American forces had taken Malolos in March and pushed north through central Luzon. In November, the Filipino army's leaders decided to break up into small guerrilla units, and President Emilio Aguinaldo fled north into the Cordillera mountains with a small party.[2]

American columns were close behind him. If Aguinaldo were captured, the Republic's government would collapse.

## The boy general

Among the officers protecting him was Gregorio del Pilar, a brigadier general only 24 years old. Born in Bulakan, Bulacan, in 1875, he was a nephew of the propagandist Marcelo H. del Pilar. He had joined the revolution as a teenager, fought in many battles against the Spanish and the Americans, and become one of the youngest generals of the revolutionary army.[1][2]

He was brave, dashing and popular, known for his fine uniforms and his love of horses. His men called him "Goyo."[1]

## Holding the pass

Aguinaldo's route north crossed the mountains of Ilocos Sur by a steep, narrow trail through Tirad Pass, more than 1,300 meters above sea level. To slow the Americans, Aguinaldo asked del Pilar to stay behind with a rearguard and hold the pass.[1][2]

Del Pilar had about sixty men. They built stone barricades along the trail where it climbed between cliffs, a position where a few soldiers could stop a much larger force.[2][3]

## The second of December

On the morning of 2 December 1899, American troops of the 33rd Volunteer Infantry, led by Major Peyton C. March, reached the foot of the pass. Accounts give their number as anywhere from about three hundred to five hundred.[2][3]

The Filipinos opened fire from their barricades, and for hours the Americans could not get through. Then an Igorot guide showed the Americans a hidden path that led around and above the Filipino positions. American soldiers climbed it and began firing down on the defenders from behind.[2][3]

The barricades fell one by one. Del Pilar was shot in the neck and killed. Of his sixty men, about fifty-two died. The Americans lost only a few men killed and wounded.[2][3]

## The last diary

Del Pilar is said to have kept a diary in his last days. An entry attributed to him, written the night before the battle, speaks of being chosen by the general to defend the pass and of knowing what that meant.[2]

Whether every word was written by him has been questioned, as with many famous last words. But the story shows how quickly his stand became a legend, among Filipinos and even among the American soldiers who fought him.[2][3]

## What the stand achieved

The battle bought Aguinaldo several hours, and he escaped into the mountains. He stayed free for more than a year longer, until he was captured in Palanan, Isabela, in March 1901.[2]

Historians weigh the battle differently. Some see it as a heroic sacrifice that kept the Republic's leader free. Others, more critical, see it as a tragic waste of young lives for a cause that was already lost, as the leadership retreated with no clear plan.[3] Both views can be true at once.

## Remembered as a young hero

American soldiers found del Pilar's body on the trail and stripped it of its valuables. Days later, an American officer buried him.[2] His remains were later moved and honored.

Del Pilar became one of the most famous heroes of the war against the Americans: the young general who chose to stand and fight. In 1955 the town of Concepción, Ilocos Sur, near the battle site, was renamed Gregorio del Pilar. The Philippine Military Academy's home in Baguio is called Fort del Pilar in his honor.[1][2]

His story was retold in the 2018 film "Goyo: Ang Batang Heneral," which brought the battle to a new generation, and which, like the historians, showed both his courage and his flaws.[3]
