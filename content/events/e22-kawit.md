## A leader in exile

By the end of 1897, the revolution against Spain had reached a stalemate. In December, under the Pact of Biak-na-Bato, Emilio Aguinaldo and other revolutionary leaders agreed to stop fighting and leave the country in exchange for money and promises of reform. They sailed to Hong Kong.[1]

Then the world changed around them. In April 1898, the United States and Spain went to war over Cuba. On 1 May, an American squadron under Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay in a single morning.[1][3]

## The return

American officers in Asia believed Filipino rebels could help them against Spain. Aguinaldo came home on 19 May 1898, brought by an American ship. Revolutionary forces quickly rose again across Luzon, and Spanish garrisons fell or surrendered one after another.[1]

On 28 May, at the Battle of Alapan in Imus, Cavite, Filipino troops won a victory, and the new Philippine flag was raised in battle for the first time. That date is now National Flag Day.[2]

Aguinaldo believed independence had to be declared quickly, before the Americans and the Spanish decided the islands' future without Filipinos. He chose his family's house in Kawit, then called Cavite el Viejo, for the ceremony.[1]

## Twelve June, 1898

On the afternoon of 12 June 1898, a large crowd gathered outside the house. Ambrosio Rianzares Bautista, a lawyer and Aguinaldo's adviser on war matters, read aloud the declaration, which he had written. It proclaimed that the Filipino people were "free and independent" of Spain.[1][3]

The flag was formally displayed to the people. It had been sewn in Hong Kong by Marcela Agoncillo, her daughter Lorenza, and Delfina Herbosa de Natividad, a niece of José Rizal. Its white triangle, red and blue fields, sun with eight rays and three stars carried meanings the revolutionaries spelled out in the declaration.[1][2]

The band of San Francisco de Malabon played a march composed by Julián Felipe, a music teacher from Cavite. Then called the Marcha Filipina Magdalo, it was later renamed the Marcha Nacional Filipina and, with words added afterward, became the national anthem.[3][4]

## What the flag meant

The declaration explained the flag's symbols. The white triangle stood for the Katipunan, whose emblem was a triangle. The eight rays of the sun stood for the eight provinces that first rose in revolt in 1896: Manila, Cavite, Bulacan, Pampanga, Nueva Ecija, Bataan, Laguna and Batangas. The three stars stood for Luzon, Mindanao and Panay.[1][2]

Today the three stars are usually explained as Luzon, the Visayas and Mindanao, and the colors as peace (white), courage (red) and unity and justice (blue).[2] The flag has kept the same basic design ever since.

## Ninety-eight signatures

The declaration was signed by 98 people, mostly military officers and leading townsmen. One of them was an American, an artillery officer named L. M. Johnson, who had come to observe.[1]

The text also contained a line that later troubled many readers: it described the new nation as under the "protection" of the "mighty and humane North American nation." To Aguinaldo's government, it was a gesture of gratitude and an appeal for support. Critics later saw it as a sign of how much the revolution was relying on the United States.[1]

## Independence that waited

Neither Spain nor the United States recognized the declaration. In December 1898, under the Treaty of Paris, Spain handed the islands to the United States, and within two months Filipinos and Americans were at war.[1][3]

For decades, the Philippines marked independence on 4 July, the day the United States recognized it in 1946. In 1962, President Diosdado Macapagal moved Independence Day to 12 June, honoring the declaration at Kawit, and a 1964 law made the change permanent.[3]

## The house in Kawit

The Aguinaldo house is now the Aguinaldo Shrine, a national monument. Aguinaldo himself lived there until his death in 1964 at the age of 94. Visitors can see the balcony, added after the declaration, where the scene of 12 June is traditionally pictured.[3]
