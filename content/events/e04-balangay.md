## Under the flood mud

Butuan sits near the mouth of the Agusan River in northeastern Mindanao. The river brings rich soil down from the mountains and, in the rainy season, floods the low ground around the city. Over many centuries, those floods buried whatever lay in the riverside marshes under thick layers of mud.

In 1976, people digging in Barangay Libertad for buried treasure hit wood. About one and a half meters down they found the remains of a large wooden boat. It was not treasure in the way they had hoped, but to archaeologists it was something far more valuable.[1][2]

{{photo:2}}

## Why the wood survived

Wood usually rots in the tropics within a few years. The Butuan boats survived because they were sealed in wet, airless mud. Without oxygen, the fungi and bacteria that break down wood cannot do their work, so the timbers stayed waterlogged but whole for many centuries.[2]

That same water makes them fragile once they come out of the ground. As waterlogged wood dries, it shrinks and cracks. Conservators have to keep excavated planks wet and treat them slowly so that they keep their shape, which is one reason only some of the boats found have been lifted.[2]

## The National Museum steps in

Archaeologists from the National Museum of the Philippines came to examine the site. Over the following years they found the remains of several more boats in the same area. They examined seven and recovered three.[2]

The boats are known as balangay, an old word for a boat that also became the name for a community, the barangay, because a boatload of families could form a settlement. The Butuan finds gave that word a physical shape.[1]

{{photo:3}}

## How to build a boat without nails

The balangay were built with a method used across island Southeast Asia. Builders shaped long planks from single trees and joined them edge to edge with wooden dowels. Raised lugs carved on the inside of each plank were lashed to ribs with plant fiber, holding the hull together. There were no iron nails.[2]

The largest of these boats were about 15 meters long and could carry dozens of people along with cargo. They could be paddled or sailed, and they were strong enough for long trips between islands.[1]

## How old are they?

Radiocarbon tests on the first boats gave a date of about 320 AD for one of them and about 1250 AD for another. That made the oldest the earliest known pre-European watercraft found in the Philippines.[1]

Later research has added care to those numbers. A detailed study of the boats by the archaeologist Bobby Orillaneda noted that the early tests in the 1970s and 1980s gave widely different results, and set out to date the timbers more reliably.[2] What is clear is that boats like these were being built and used in Butuan over many centuries.

## A port of gold and trade

The boats were not the only treasures from Butuan. The same area has yielded gold ornaments, imported Chinese porcelain and goods from mainland Southeast Asia. Chinese records mention envoys from a place they called Butuan arriving at the Song court in the early 1000s.[1]

Archaeologists have also found the tools of a working town in Butuan: remains of metalworking, glass beads and pottery made both locally and abroad.[1]

Together, these finds describe a busy trading port with links across the South China Sea long before Spanish ships arrived. The balangay were the vehicles of that world, carrying people, goods and ideas between the islands and beyond.

## Where to see them

One of the boats is displayed where it was found, at the Balangay Shrine Museum in Libertad, Butuan City. Another is kept at the Maritime Hall of the National Museum in Manila.[2] In 2009, Filipino sailors built a modern replica using traditional methods and sailed it across the seas of Southeast Asia, showing how far such boats could go.
