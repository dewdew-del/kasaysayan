## The secret is out

By August 1896 the Katipunan had thousands of members, and keeping it secret was getting harder. On 19 August, a member named Teodoro Patiño told his sister about the society after a quarrel at the printing shop where both worked. She told a nun, and the story reached the Spanish parish priest of Tondo, Father Mariano Gil.[1][3]

Gil went to the police. The shop was searched, and Katipunan papers, receipts and a printing stone were found. Within days, Spanish authorities began arresting suspected members across Manila.[1]

## Into the hills

Andrés Bonifacio and other leaders slipped out of the city. With hundreds of Katipuneros they gathered in the countryside north of Manila, in what was then the town of Caloocan, among the farms and ravines around Balintawak and the settlements nearby.[1]

There, in a series of meetings in the last days of August, the leaders argued about what to do. Some wanted to wait until they had more weapons. Bonifacio insisted that the time had come: with the society discovered, there was no going back.[1][3]

## Torn papers

At one of these meetings, the Katipuneros tore up their cédulas personales, the identity and tax certificates that every colonial subject had to carry and pay for. It was a public and deliberate break with Spain. Shouts of "Long live the Philippines!" rang out.[1][2]

For the farmers and workers who did it, tearing the cédula was a real risk. Without it, a person could be arrested on sight. There was no way to pretend afterward that they had not chosen a side.[2]

This moment is remembered as the Cry, or Sigaw: the Cry of Pugad Lawin in today's official history, and for many years before that, the Cry of Balintawak.

## Which place, which day?

The trouble is that the people who were there remembered it differently, and most wrote down their memories decades later.[2][3]

Pío Valenzuela, a doctor and Katipunan leader, said the cédulas were torn on 23 August at the house of Juan Ramos, Melchora Aquino's son, in a place called Pugad Lawin. Santiago Alvarez, a Katipunan general, wrote that the Cry happened at Bahay Toro on 24 August. Gregoria de Jesús, Bonifacio's widow, and others placed it in Balintawak or elsewhere in Caloocan around 25 or 26 August. Another member, Guillermo Masangkay, gave Balintawak on 26 August, and later changed his account.[2][3]

From 1908, the government observed the "Cry of Balintawak" on 26 August, and a monument was built there. In 1956 the historian Teodoro Agoncillo, relying on Valenzuela, argued for Pugad Lawin on 23 August, and in 1963 the government adopted that version.[1][3]

## The government's own doubts

Even official bodies have not treated the matter as closed. The National Commission for Culture and the Arts has noted that the 23 August version "cannot be accepted as historically accurate" without more documents, because it rests so heavily on a single witness who changed parts of his story.[1]

This page shows the official date and place, while marking them as contested. What every account agrees on is the heart of it: in the last week of August 1896, near Caloocan, the Katipunan publicly chose revolution.

## The fighting begins

The first clashes came within days. On 30 August 1896, Bonifacio led an attack on the Spanish powder magazine at San Juan del Monte, in what is remembered as the Battle of Pinaglabanan. The Katipuneros were driven back with heavy losses.[1]

The same day, the governor-general declared a state of war in eight provinces: Manila, Bulacan, Pampanga, Nueva Ecija, Tarlac, Laguna, Cavite and Batangas. The eight rays of the sun on the Philippine flag honor the first provinces to rise. The 1898 declaration of independence lists them slightly differently, with Bataan in place of Tarlac.[1]

The revolution spread fastest in Cavite, where Katipunan leaders, among them a young town mayor named Emilio Aguinaldo, won a string of victories. It was the beginning of the struggle that would lead to the declaration of independence two years later.
