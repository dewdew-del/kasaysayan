## A country in ruins

The Philippines came out of the Second World War devastated. Manila was one of the most heavily damaged cities of the war. Roads, bridges, ports, farms, schools and factories had been destroyed across the islands. More than a million Filipinos are estimated to have died during the war years.[2][4]

Still, the date set in the Tydings–McDuffie Act of 1934 held. Independence would come on 4 July 1946.

{{photo:2}}

## The first president of the Republic

In April 1946, Filipinos voted for a president. Manuel Roxas, a former senator and Speaker of the House who had served in Bataan and under the occupation government, ran as the candidate of a new Liberal Party. He defeated Sergio Osmeña, who had led the Commonwealth since Quezon's death.[2]

Roxas had the support of General Douglas MacArthur, who defended him against accusations that he had collaborated with the Japanese.[2]

## Fourth of July at the Luneta

On the morning of 4 July 1946, a huge crowd filled the Luneta, the park by Manila Bay where José Rizal had been executed fifty years earlier. People came from all over the ruined city and beyond.[1][3]

At the ceremony, the American flag was lowered for the last time and the Philippine flag was raised alone. The American high commissioner, Paul V. McNutt, read the proclamation of independence. Roxas took his oath as the first president of the Republic of the Philippines.[1][3]

That same day, the two countries signed the Treaty of Manila, in which the United States gave up its sovereignty over the islands, and President Harry Truman issued a proclamation recognizing Philippine independence.[1]

## Independence with conditions

Independence came with strings attached. The US Congress tied money for rebuilding the war-ravaged country to a trade law, the Bell Trade Act. It required the Philippines to give American citizens "parity rights": the same rights as Filipinos to own land and exploit natural resources such as mines and forests.[2][4]

Because the 1935 Constitution reserved those rights for Filipinos, giving them to Americans meant amending it. After a bitter fight, in which some opposition members of Congress were removed from their seats, the amendment passed in a 1947 plebiscite.[2]

The Bell Act also tied the Philippine peso to the US dollar.[2] In 1947, a Military Bases Agreement allowed the United States to keep military bases in the Philippines, including Clark Air Base and Subic Naval Base, for 99 years. The term was later shortened, and the bases left in the 1990s.[2]

## A celebration under ruins

The crowd at the Luneta celebrated amid destruction. Many had lost family members in the war. Some had walked past the shattered buildings of Intramuros and Ermita to reach the park.[3]

Witnesses later remembered the mix of emotions: joy at seeing the Philippine flag fly alone, sadness for those who had not lived to see it, and uncertainty about what independence would mean for a country that still depended so heavily on the United States.[3]

## A new republic

The new government faced huge problems: rebuilding, feeding people, paying war debts and dealing with a peasant rebellion in Central Luzon. Roxas died suddenly of a heart attack in 1948 and was succeeded by his vice president, Elpidio Quirino.[2] In its first years, the Republic also joined the new United Nations as a founding member and began building its own foreign service, embassies and armed forces.[2]

## Which day?

For years, the Philippines celebrated independence on 4 July. In 1962, President Diosdado Macapagal moved Independence Day to 12 June, the day the revolutionaries had declared independence from Spain in 1898. Today 4 July is celebrated as Philippine–American Friendship Day.[2][3]

The change reflected a long debate about what independence meant. Was it given by the United States in 1946, or won by Filipinos in 1898? Many historians answer: both, and neither date tells the whole story.[2]
