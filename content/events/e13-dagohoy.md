## An island under the friars

By the 1700s Bohol had been under Spanish rule for more than a century. As in much of the colony, the most powerful Spaniards in most towns were not soldiers or officials but parish priests. In Bohol these were first Jesuits, who ran the island's missions until the order was expelled from Spain's lands in 1768.[1]

Parish priests collected church dues, organized forced labor and enforced religious rules. Filipinos also paid tribute to the state. For many people in Bohol's hilly interior, these demands were heavy, and resentment grew.[1][3]

## A brother left unburied

The revolt is traditionally said to have begun with a death. Francisco Sendrijas, known as Dagohoy, was a cabeza de barangay, a local leader, in the town of Inabanga. His brother, a constable, was killed while trying to arrest a man who had left the church. The Jesuit priest refused to give the brother a Christian burial, on the grounds that he had died in a duel. The body was left to rot.[1][3]

This story comes from later tradition, and historians use it with some caution. What is documented is that in January 1744 a Jesuit priest in the area, Father Giuseppe Lamberti, was killed, and that a widespread revolt followed.[1][2]

## Free in the hills

Dagohoy and his followers withdrew to the mountains around what is now the town of Danao, in the interior of Bohol. Thousands of people joined them, abandoning the lowland towns. They set up their own communities, farmed their own land and refused to pay tribute or obey Spanish priests.[1][3]

The mountains were well suited to resistance. The rough limestone country around Danao, full of caves and narrow valleys, was hard for outsiders to enter and easy for local people to defend.[1]

{{photo:3}}

## Twenty governors

The Spanish tried many times to end the revolt. Expeditions were sent, missionaries tried to persuade the rebels to come down, and offers of pardon were made. According to histories of the revolt, some twenty successive governors-general held office while it went on, and none of them managed to restore full Spanish control of the rebel areas.[1]

The rebels held out long after Dagohoy himself was gone. Accounts of his death differ: some say he died in battle, others that he died peacefully of old age in his mountain stronghold.[1][3] His followers continued the struggle for generations.

## Life in the rebel communities

Little is written about daily life in the rebel settlements, because most of the records were made by the Spanish trying to defeat them. What those records show is a society that could feed itself, defend itself and carry on for generations, with its own leaders and its own sense of what it was fighting for.[1]

Their leaders had to settle disputes, organize farming and defense, and decide how to answer the missionaries who came into the hills to persuade them to return. For three generations they chose to stay.[1]

## The end, 85 years later

It was not until the 1820s that the Spanish committed enough troops to end the revolt. A large campaign under the Spanish officer José Lázaro Cairo, aided by forces from Cebu and elsewhere, pushed into the interior. The revolt was finally declared over on 31 August 1829.[2]

By then it had lasted about 85 years. Many of the surviving rebels were resettled in new towns in the lowlands, some of which still exist in Bohol today.[1][2]

## Remembered in Bohol

The Dagohoy revolt is remembered as the longest revolt in Philippine history.[1] It shows how resistance to colonial rule did not begin in 1896, but took many forms over centuries, from Mactan in 1521 to the hills of Bohol in the 1700s.

The town of Dagohoy in Bohol is named after him, and a marker in Danao recalls the mountain refuge where his followers lived free for generations.[3]
