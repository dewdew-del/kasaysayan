## Land and debt

Central Luzon, the great rice plain north of Manila, was a land of tenant farmers. Most families did not own the fields they worked. They rented land from large landowners, gave them a big share of the harvest and often fell deep into debt.[2]

In the 1930s, peasant unions and the Socialist and Communist parties organized these farmers to demand better shares, lower interest and fair treatment. Strikes and clashes with landlords and police were common. Among the organizers was Luis Taruc, a young man from San Luis, Pampanga, who had studied briefly in Manila before returning to work with the unions.[1][2]

## War comes to the rice fields

When Japan invaded in December 1941, the unions' leaders decided to fight. They saw the occupation as a threat both to the nation and to the gains peasants had won.[2]

On 29 March 1942, while the defenders of Bataan were still holding out, about 300 peasant and union leaders met in a forest clearing where the provinces of Pampanga, Tarlac and Nueva Ecija meet. They founded the Hukbong Bayan Laban sa mga Hapon, the "People's Army Against the Japanese," known as the Hukbalahap or Huks.[2][4] Accounts differ slightly on the exact place and on whether it was 28 or 29 March.[1][3]

Taruc was chosen as commander. His deputy was Casto Alejandrino. One of the first squadron leaders was a woman, Felipa Culala, known as Dayang-Dayang.[2][4]

## A people's army

The Huks were different from most guerrilla groups. They were organized from the villages up, and their fighters were mostly farmers who worked their fields by day and fought by night. Villagers fed them, hid them and passed them information.[2]

They fought Japanese soldiers and the Filipino police and officials who worked with them. They also set up local governments in the areas they controlled, collected taxes, held trials and pushed landlords to give tenants a fairer share.[2][4] Some of the landlords had fled or collaborated with the Japanese, so the Huks often took over their lands.

{{photo:3}}

## Women in the movement

Women played a large role in the Huks. Besides fighters like Felipa Culala, many women served as couriers, carrying messages and supplies through Japanese checkpoints, as nurses, as organizers in the villages and as political teachers.[2][4]

For some, the movement offered roles that village life had never allowed. Several women rose to positions of command, and their stories have been recovered in later memoirs and studies of the resistance.[3]

## Strong, and distrusted

By the end of the war, the Huks had thousands of armed fighters and claimed to have killed thousands of Japanese and collaborators.[4] They had freed large parts of Central Luzon before American forces arrived.

But they were not trusted by the Americans or by the old Philippine elite. The US Army's own guerrilla network saw them as communists and rivals. When American troops returned in 1945, they disarmed some Huk units and arrested their leaders, including Taruc and Alejandrino, for a time.[2][4]

## From guerrillas to rebels

After the war, Taruc and other Huk-backed candidates were elected to Congress in 1946. But they were not allowed to take their seats, accused of fraud and violence. Landlords returned with private armies, and peasant leaders were killed.[2]

The Huks went back to the hills. Renamed the Hukbong Mapagpalaya ng Bayan, the "People's Liberation Army," they fought the Philippine government in a rebellion that lasted until the mid-1950s.[2][4]

## How they are remembered

For decades, the Huks were remembered mainly as communist rebels. More recently, historians and government bodies have recognized their role in the war against Japan. In 2017, a government body recognized Luis Taruc as a hero of the resistance.[1]

The Huk story is a reminder that the war against Japan was also a war over land, and that the questions of who owns the fields of Central Luzon did not end when the war did.[2]
