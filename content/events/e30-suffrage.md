## A long campaign

The fight for women's votes in the Philippines began early in the American period. In 1905, a group of women in Manila founded the Asociación Feminista Filipina, among the first women's organizations in the country, to work on education, health and women's rights.[2]

Through the 1910s and 1920s, Filipino women's groups lobbied the legislature again and again. Bills to give women the vote were introduced several times, and each time they failed. Many male politicians argued that women belonged at home, that politics would corrupt them, or that they would simply vote as their husbands or priests told them.[2]

## A clause in the constitution

When the 1935 Constitution was written, women's suffrage was one of its most argued-over questions. The delegates, all men, reached a compromise. The constitution did not give women the vote outright. Instead, it said women would gain the right if, within two years, at least 300,000 women voted for it in a special plebiscite.[1][2]

It was a high bar. Only women could vote in this plebiscite, and they had to register first. The country's suffragists had two years to organize.

## The campaign

Women's groups launched a nationwide campaign. They traveled to towns and provinces, held meetings, wrote in newspapers and spoke on the radio, encouraging women to register and vote "yes."[2][3]

Leaders of the movement included women such as Pura Villanueva Kalaw, Concepción Felix-Rodríguez and Encarnación Alzona, a historian who later became the first Filipina to earn a doctorate. They had support from some male politicians, including President Manuel L. Quezon, who backed the cause.[2][3]

The National Assembly set the details in Commonwealth Act No. 34, passed in 1936, which scheduled the plebiscite for 30 April 1937.[4]

## The arguments for and against

Opponents of women's suffrage made several arguments. Some said that voting would take women away from their families and that politics was a dirty business unfit for them. Others feared that women would be easily influenced by the Church, or by their husbands, and would not vote independently.[2]

Suffragists answered that women already worked, paid taxes, ran households and businesses, and taught in the new public schools. They argued that a democracy that excluded half its people was not a real democracy, and that women would bring honesty and care for families into public life.[2][3]

## Voting day

On 30 April 1937, women across the Philippines went to the polls. The result was overwhelming: about 447,725 women voted "yes," far more than the 300,000 required. Only a small number voted "no."[1][3]

With that vote, Filipino women won the right to vote and to run for office in national elections.

## Women in office

Women voted and ran in the elections that followed. In 1941, Elisa Rosales Ochoa of Agusan was elected to the National Assembly, becoming the first woman in the Philippine legislature.[1][3] In 1946, Geronima Pecson became the first woman elected to the Senate.[3]

In the decades since, Filipino women have held almost every office in the country, including the presidency: Corazon Aquino in 1986 and Gloria Macapagal Arroyo in 2001.[3]

## Why it was early

The campaign also changed how women's groups worked. To win, they had to organize across the whole country, in many languages, reaching women in towns and villages far from Manila. That network of women's clubs and associations remained active in public life long after 1937.[2]


The Philippines was one of the first countries in Asia where women won the vote, and they won it partly by voting for it themselves. That made the 1937 plebiscite unusual: in most countries, men decided whether to extend the vote to women. In the Philippines, women were asked, and they answered.[2]

## Remembered every April

The anniversary is marked each year as a milestone for women's rights in the Philippines.[3] It is also a reminder of how recent the right to vote is for half the population, and how hard it was won.
