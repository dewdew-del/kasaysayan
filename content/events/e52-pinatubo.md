## A forgotten volcano

Before 1991, few people outside Central Luzon had heard of Mount Pinatubo. It was a forested mountain on the borders of Zambales, Tarlac and Pampanga, home to Aeta communities who farmed and hunted on its slopes. It had not erupted in about 500 years, and most maps did not even mark it as a volcano.[1][4]

Near its foot lay Clark Air Base, one of the largest American military bases in the world, and towns and cities home to hundreds of thousands of people.

## The mountain wakes

In March and April 1991, people living on the mountain felt earthquakes and saw steam bursting from its upper slopes. Scientists from the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology, PHIVOLCS, went to investigate, and were soon joined by volcanologists from the United States Geological Survey.[1][2]

Together they set up instruments to measure earthquakes, ground swelling and gases. The signs pointed to a large eruption on the way. They set up a five-level alert system and began warning local officials.[1][3]

## Getting people out

Persuading people to leave was difficult. Many had never seen an eruption and did not believe one was coming. Scientists showed films of other eruptions to officials and residents to explain the danger.[1]

As alert levels rose in early June, tens of thousands of people were evacuated, including Aeta families from the mountain slopes. On 10 June, the US military evacuated most of Clark Air Base, moving about 15,000 people to Subic Bay.[1][4]

## 15 June 1991

Smaller explosions began on 12 June. Then, on the afternoon of 15 June 1991, Pinatubo erupted with enormous force. A column of ash and gas rose about 35 kilometers into the sky. Glowing avalanches of hot ash, called pyroclastic flows, raced down the valleys around the mountain.[2][4]

It was the second-largest volcanic eruption of the 20th century, smaller only than the 1912 eruption of Novarupta in Alaska.[1][2] The top of the mountain collapsed, leaving a huge crater, or caldera, more than two kilometers across.

## A typhoon at the same moment

At the same time, by terrible chance, Typhoon Yunya, called Diding in the Philippines, passed over Luzon. Its rain fell through the ash cloud. Wet, heavy ash, like cement, came down over a huge area, and the weight caused many roofs to collapse.[1][4]

Most of the deaths on and right after 15 June came from roofs falling in under wet ash.[1]

## Lahar

The disaster did not end with the eruption. Billions of cubic meters of loose ash lay on the volcano's slopes. Every rainy season afterward, rain turned it into lahar, rivers of mud and rock that rushed down the valleys, burying farms, roads, bridges and entire towns, especially in Pampanga and Tarlac.[1][3]

Some towns, such as Bacolor in Pampanga, were buried under several meters of lahar in the years that followed.[3]

## The toll, and the lives saved

About 847 deaths are usually counted from the eruption, its roof collapses, lahars and the diseases that spread in crowded evacuation camps, though sources give different totals.[1][4] Many of the dead were Aeta, who suffered greatly in the camps, far from their land.

Yet scientists estimate that the warnings and evacuations saved thousands of lives, perhaps 5,000 or more.[1][4] Pinatubo is now studied around the world as an example of how volcano monitoring and early action can prevent a much greater catastrophe.

## A changed landscape

The eruption sent so much sulfur gas into the upper atmosphere that it cooled the whole world's average temperature by about half a degree Celsius for the next year or two.[2]

At home, it changed the history of Central Luzon. Clark Air Base was so badly damaged that the Americans abandoned it. Today the crater holds a lake, and visitors hike through the lahar valleys to see it.[4]
