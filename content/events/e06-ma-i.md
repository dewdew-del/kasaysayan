## Ships at Canton

In the year 971, according to the official history of China's Song dynasty, ships from a place called Ma-i arrived at the great southern port of Canton, today's Guangzhou. Ma-i was listed among the "southern sea" countries that came to trade.[1]

Eleven years later, in 982, Ma-i traders came again, bringing goods to sell and asking to trade under the supervision of the port officials who managed foreign ships.[1][2] These short entries are among the earliest written mentions of a place in the Philippines, and they come not from the islands themselves but from the records of a foreign empire.

## How China kept its books

The Song dynasty, which ruled China from 960, ran its overseas trade through a system of official ports. Foreign ships had to report to the Maritime Trade Office, which recorded their arrival, taxed their cargo and bought certain goods for the court.[1]

Because of this paperwork, Chinese chroniclers wrote down the names of the places foreign traders came from, what they brought and sometimes how they behaved. Ma-i appears in these records more than once, which suggests regular contact rather than a single lost ship.[1]

## What Zhao Rugua wrote

The most detailed description of Ma-i comes later, from a book called Zhufan zhi, or "A Description of Barbarian Peoples," written around 1225 by Zhao Rugua, a customs official in the port of Quanzhou.[1][2]

Zhao never visited the islands. He interviewed merchants and sailors who did. According to his account, Chinese ships trading at Ma-i anchored offshore and beat drums to announce their arrival. Local traders came out in small boats, took Chinese goods such as porcelain, silk and iron needles inland, and came back later to pay with local products, including beeswax, cotton, pearls, tortoiseshell and betel nuts.[2]

What struck Zhao most was the honesty of the trade. Goods were often handed over on credit, and the traders of Ma-i came back to pay, sometimes months later.[2]

## Where was Ma-i?

The records never give a map, so historians have had to work out where Ma-i was from its name and the routes described. The most common view places it on the island of Mindoro, where a town called Mait still exists, and where Chinese porcelain from this period has been found.[1]

Other scholars have doubted this. Some argue that Ma-i was Bay, a lakeshore town in Laguna whose older names sound similar. Another proposal linked it to a place in Borneo. The historian William Henry Scott, who collected and studied these Chinese sources, treated some of these identifications with caution.[1][3]

Zhao's book also lists other places that seem to belong to the Philippine islands, and some historians think "Ma-i" may at times have been used loosely for a wider area of the islands, not one town.[1]

## Reading records written by outsiders

Using Chinese records to learn about the Philippines comes with a catch. They were written by officials whose main concern was trade, taxes and the court, not the lives of the people they described. They often repeated information from earlier books, and they sometimes mixed up places that were far apart.[1]

That is why historians compare different Chinese texts with each other, with archaeology and with later Spanish and local accounts. When several kinds of evidence point the same way, as they do for the existence of trade between the islands and China at this time, the conclusion is much stronger than any single line in a chronicle.[1][3]

## A trading world before 1521

Whatever its exact location, Ma-i shows that people in the islands were part of a large trading network centuries before Europeans arrived. They knew how to deal with foreign merchants, set terms, extend and repay credit, and move goods between the coast and the interior.[2]

The Chinese porcelain that turns up in burial sites across the Philippines, from Luzon to Mindanao, is physical evidence of that world. The brief lines about Ma-i in the Song records are its first written trace.
