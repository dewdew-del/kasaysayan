## Seven years in prison, three in exile

Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr. had been the youngest senator in the country and the most likely to succeed Ferdinand Marcos as president when martial law was declared in 1972. He was arrested that first night and spent more than seven years in prison. In 1977 a military commission sentenced him to death.[4]

In 1980, after he suffered a heart attack in his cell, Marcos allowed him to go to the United States for surgery. He stayed there in exile with his wife, Corazon, and their children, teaching at universities and speaking against the regime.[4]

## Going home

By 1983 Aquino believed he had to return. Marcos was ill, the economy was in decline, and Aquino feared that without a peaceful opposition the country would fall into violence. He hoped to persuade Marcos to restore democracy.[2][4]

Friends and family warned him he would be killed. The government refused him a passport, so he traveled under another name. He flew from Boston by way of Taipei, with a group of journalists on board.[2]

## The tarmac

On the afternoon of Sunday, 21 August 1983, his China Airlines plane landed at Manila International Airport. Soldiers came on board and led Aquino out, down the stairs that led directly to the tarmac, out of sight of the waiting journalists.[2][4]

Seconds later, shots rang out. Aquino fell dead on the tarmac. Beside him lay another man, Rolando Galman, who was also shot dead by soldiers.[2] Thousands of supporters waiting in the terminal, with yellow ribbons, did not at first understand what had happened.

## "The Filipino is worth dying for"

Aquino had prepared a statement to read on arrival, which he never got to deliver. In it he said he had come home to join the struggle for peaceful change. A line attributed to him in the years before his death became famous: "The Filipino is worth dying for."[4]

## "A lone gunman"

The government said Galman, a communist hitman, had shot Aquino, and that soldiers had then killed Galman. Very few people believed it.[1][3]

## The funeral

Aquino's body lay in state at his family's home and then at Santo Domingo Church in Quezon City. His mother insisted that his bloodied clothes not be changed, so that people could see what had been done to him.[4]

On 31 August, his funeral procession took about eleven hours to reach the cemetery. Estimates of the crowds that lined the route ran into the millions. It was the largest gathering the country had seen in years.[2][4]

## The Agrava Board

Under pressure, Marcos created an independent fact-finding board, led by retired judge Corazon Agrava. In October 1984, the board issued its reports. Both the majority and minority reports rejected the lone-gunman story and concluded that soldiers had conspired to kill Aquino. The majority report named the armed forces chief, General Fabian Ver, among the people responsible; Agrava's own report did not.[1]

In December 1985, a special court acquitted Ver and all the accused soldiers.[3]

## A retrial

After Marcos fell in 1986, the case was reopened. In September 1990, the Sandiganbayan convicted 16 soldiers, including Brigadier General Luther Custodio, of the murders of Aquino and Galman, and sentenced them to life imprisonment. The Supreme Court upheld the verdict.[3][4]

The court found that soldiers, not Galman, had shot Aquino. But it did not identify who had ordered the killing. That question has never been answered in court.[3]

## The beginning of the end

Aquino's death changed the country. People who had never protested before now marched, often wearing yellow, the color of his campaign. Business leaders, students, nuns and ordinary families joined weekly rallies.[2][4]

His widow, Corazon Aquino, who had never held office, became the face of the opposition. Two and a half years later, she ran against Marcos for president. Today 21 August is a national holiday, Ninoy Aquino Day, and Manila's airport is named after him.[4]
