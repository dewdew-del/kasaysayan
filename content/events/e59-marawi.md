## The Islamic City

Marawi sits on the northern shore of Lake Lanao in the highlands of Mindanao. It is the capital of Lanao del Sur and the largest Muslim city in the Philippines, sometimes called the "Islamic City." Most of its people are Maranao, known for their trade, crafts and traditions.[3]

In the 2010s, small armed groups in the region declared loyalty to the so-called Islamic State in the Middle East. Among them were the Maute group, led by brothers from Lanao, and a faction of the Abu Sayyaf led by Isnilon Hapilon, whom the Islamic State had named its leader in Southeast Asia.[1][4]

{{photo:2}}

## 23 May 2017

On 23 May 2017, government troops raided a house in Marawi to capture Hapilon. They met far more fighters than they expected. Hundreds of militants, many of them local and some foreign, came out across the city. They burned buildings, took hostages, including a Catholic priest, and raised black flags.[1][4]

That night President Rodrigo Duterte declared martial law across all of Mindanao.[1]

## A city emptied

Within days, almost the entire population of Marawi fled. Hundreds of thousands of people left their homes, staying in evacuation centers and with relatives in nearby towns and in Iligan.[2][3]

For many families, it was the start of years of displacement.

{{photo:3}}

## Five months of fighting

The militants held the center of the old city, a maze of narrow streets, concrete houses, shops and mosques along the lake. They used tunnels, snipers and hostages. The armed forces fought them house by house, supported by airstrikes and artillery.[1][4]

It was the longest urban battle in modern Philippine history. As weeks turned into months, the center of Marawi, which residents later called "ground zero," was reduced to ruins.[2][4]

## The end of the siege

On 16 October 2017, Isnilon Hapilon and Omarkhayam Maute were killed in a military operation. The next day President Duterte declared Marawi "liberated." The last militants were killed or captured, and on 23 October the defense secretary announced that combat operations were over.[1]

## Life in the evacuation centers

For the displaced, the months of fighting were spent in crowded evacuation centers, schools and relatives' homes. Families who had run with only the clothes they wore waited for news of their houses and neighbors.[2][3]

Many Maranao families also lost their livelihoods, since the shops and markets of the old city were the heart of their trade. Aid groups, mosques, churches and volunteers from across Mindanao brought food, water and medicine.[3]

## The cost

According to government figures, more than 900 militants and about 165 soldiers and police were killed, along with dozens of civilians; some reports put the total dead above a thousand. More than a thousand hostages and trapped civilians were rescued.[1][3]

About 360,000 people were displaced at the height of the crisis.[3] Thousands of homes, shops, schools and mosques were destroyed, along with centuries-old Maranao houses and family heirlooms that cannot be replaced.

## Rebuilding Marawi

The government set up Task Force Bangon Marawi to rebuild the city. Work in the most damaged area began only in late 2018, after delays.[2][3]

Rebuilding was slow, and many residents were frustrated that they could not return to their land for years, or were not consulted on plans for their own city. Roads, a new mosque, public buildings and a museum were built, but many families remained in temporary shelters long after the siege.[2][4]

## What it means

The siege showed how violent extremism could take root where poverty, weak government and old grievances meet. It also showed the resilience of the Maranao people, who lost their homes and much of their city but not their identity or their faith.[4]

Each year, residents and officials mark the anniversaries with prayers and reflections, and with continued calls for the full return of Marawi to its people.[2]
