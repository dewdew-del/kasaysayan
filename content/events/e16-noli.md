## A student far from home

In the 1880s, José Rizal was one of many young Filipinos studying in Europe. Born in Calamba, Laguna, in 1861, he had studied in Manila and then sailed to Spain in 1882, where he studied medicine and philosophy in Madrid. Later he trained as an eye doctor in Paris and Heidelberg.[3]

Rizal and other Filipino students in Europe, later known as the propagandists, wanted reforms in the colony: representation in the Spanish parliament, freedom of speech, equal treatment under the law and an end to the power of the friars over Filipino towns. They wrote essays and newspapers. Rizal decided to write a novel.[3]

## Writing the Noli

He began the book in Madrid in 1884, continued it in Paris and finished it in Germany in early 1887. He called it Noli Me Tangere, Latin for "Touch me not," words from the Gospel of John that Rizal used to suggest a wound too painful to touch: the "social cancer" of the colony.[3]

The novel tells the story of Crisóstomo Ibarra, a young Filipino who comes home from Europe full of hope, and of the people around him in a small town: the corrupt friar Padre Dámaso, the gentle María Clara, the rebel Elías, the mad mother Sisa and her lost sons. Through them, Rizal showed the cruelty, hypocrisy and injustice he saw in colonial society.[3]

## A loan from a friend

Rizal was nearly broke and could not pay a printer. His friend Maximo Viola, a doctor from Bulacan, lent him the money to print 2,000 copies, about 300 pesos.[1]

The book was printed in Berlin by a printing company called the Berliner Buchdruckerei-Actien-Gesellschaft. It came off the press in March 1887. Sources give slightly different dates, around 21 to 29 March.[1][2] Rizal gave Viola the first copy, along with the galley proofs and the pen he had used.[1]

## Banned and smuggled

Rizal sent copies to friends in the Philippines and around the world. In Manila, the book caused an uproar. Friars condemned it, and a commission of church and government officials declared it subversive. Owning it became dangerous.[2][3]

Copies were smuggled into the colony and passed from hand to hand. People read it in secret. Its characters became familiar to Filipinos who had never left their own towns, and its picture of colonial life rang true to them.[3]

## Why it struck so deep

Part of the Noli's power was that it was funny as well as angry. Rizal mocked the pompous, the greedy and the foolish, Spanish and Filipino alike, in scenes that readers recognized from their own towns. He also wrote with great tenderness about ordinary people who suffered under the system.[3]

The book was written in Spanish, the language of the educated elite, so its first readers were mostly ilustrados like Rizal himself. But its story spread far beyond them, retold and discussed by people who could not read it themselves.[3]

## Coming home

When Rizal returned to the Philippines later in 1887, he found himself famous and under suspicion. The governor-general assigned a Spanish lieutenant to guard him. He soon left again for Hong Kong, Japan, the United States and Europe.[3]

In 1891 he published a darker sequel, El Filibusterismo, dedicated to the three priests executed in 1872.[3] The two novels made him the most famous Filipino of his time, and the colonial government's most wanted critic.

## The book every student reads

Rizal did not call for armed revolution in the Noli. He wanted reform. But the book helped many Filipinos imagine themselves as a people with shared grievances and a shared future, and that idea was revolutionary in itself.[3]

Today, under a 1956 law known as the Rizal Law, every Filipino college student studies Rizal's life and works, including Noli Me Tangere.[3] More than 135 years after a borrowed 300 pesos paid for the first printing, it remains one of the most important books in Philippine history.
