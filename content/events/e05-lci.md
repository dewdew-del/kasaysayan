## Found in the sand

Around 1989, a man dredging sand near the mouth of the Lumbang River, where it empties into Laguna de Bay, pulled up a thin, folded sheet of copper. It was about the size of a sheet of paper and covered on one side with lines of small, neat writing that nobody around him could read.[1]

Like many objects that come out of the ground by chance, it was sold. It passed through the hands of an antiques dealer before reaching the National Museum of the Philippines in Manila. There it waited, a small object with a very large question attached: what did it say?[1]

## A reader from Mindoro

The answer came from Antoon Postma, a Dutch anthropologist who had spent decades among the Mangyan people of Mindoro, studying their old scripts and poetry. Postma recognized the writing on the plate as a form of Kawi, a script used in Java and other parts of island Southeast Asia in the first millennium.[1]

He spent months working through it letter by letter and in 1992 published the first full reading in the journal Philippine Studies.[1] His work turned a curious piece of metal into a document that could speak.

## A date carved in copper

The plate begins with a date in the Saka calendar, the calendar used in much of South and Southeast Asia at the time. It names the year 822, the month of Waisaka and the fourth day of the waning moon. Converted to our calendar, that is 21 April 900 AD.[1][2]

That made the Laguna Copperplate Inscription the oldest known written document from the Philippines. It is more than six centuries older than Antonio Pigafetta's account of Magellan's voyage, which for a long time was treated as the start of the islands' written history.[2]

## A debt is cleared

The text is a legal record. It states that a woman named Angkatan and her brother Bukah, children of a man named Namwaran, are released from a debt owed by their father. The amount is given in gold, one kati and eight suwarna, which scholars estimate at somewhere between about 865 and 926 grams.[1][2]

The release was granted by a high official, and the plate lists his title and the witnesses who were present, so that no one could claim the debt again in the future. In other words, it is a receipt, written to be kept.[1]

## Places with familiar names

The inscription mentions several places. Postma read their names as Tundun, Pailah, Puliran and Binwangan. Tundun is usually linked to Tondo, on Manila Bay. Pailah has been linked to Pila in Laguna, although some scholars place it, and Binwangan, in present-day Bulacan.[1][2]

The language is mostly Old Malay, the trade language of the region, mixed with words from Sanskrit, Old Javanese and an early form of Tagalog.[2] That mix fits a world where traders, officials and scribes moved between the islands of Southeast Asia and borrowed words and ideas from each other.

## What it tells us

Before the plate was read, many textbooks described the pre-colonial islands as a world without writing, government or law. The inscription shows something very different: rulers with titles, a system of debt and repayment, standard weights of gold, scribes trained in a regional script, and ties to the kingdoms of Java and beyond.[2]

It doesn't tell us everything. It is a single document, and there is still debate about some of its words and place names. But it opened a door that had seemed closed.

## Where to see it

The original plate is kept at the National Museum of Anthropology in Manila.[2] It is small enough to overlook, a thin sheet of metal in a glass case, but it holds the oldest words written on these islands that anyone has found so far.
