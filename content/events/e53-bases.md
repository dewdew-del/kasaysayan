## Bases from another century

American military bases had been in the Philippines since the colonial period. After independence in 1946, a Military Bases Agreement signed in 1947 let the United States keep them, originally for 99 years. The term was later shortened, and the agreement was set to expire in September 1991.[1][4]

The two biggest were Clark Air Base in Pampanga and the Subic Bay Naval Base in Zambales, the largest American naval base outside the United States. They were used in the Korean and Vietnam wars, and tens of thousands of Filipinos worked on or around them.[1]

## A constitution's condition

The 1987 Constitution said that after the old agreement expired, foreign military bases could stay only under a treaty approved by two-thirds of the Senate, that is, 16 of 24 senators.[3]

The United States and the Aquino government negotiated a new treaty, the Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Security. It would let the US Navy stay at Subic for another ten years in exchange for aid. Clark was not included: it had been badly damaged by the eruption of Mount Pinatubo in June 1991, and the Americans had decided to give it up.[1][2]

## A divided country

The debate split the country. Supporters of the treaty said the bases brought jobs, money and security, especially at a time when the economy was weak and Pinatubo had just devastated Central Luzon. President Corazon Aquino supported it and even led a march in its favor.[1]

Opponents said the bases were a leftover of colonial rule, made the country a target in any war, and brought social problems such as prostitution around the bases. They argued that true independence meant no foreign troops on Philippine soil.[3][4]

## The protests

Both sides took to the streets. Nationalist groups, students and left-wing organizations held rallies demanding the bases leave. In the days before the vote, President Aquino led a march of supporters in the rain to show public backing for the treaty.[1]

Outside the Senate, crowds gathered on both sides as senators made long speeches explaining their votes, many of them emotional, many recalling the country's colonial past.[1][3]

## The vote

On 16 September 1991, the Senate voted. Twelve senators voted to reject the treaty and eleven to approve it. Since approval required sixteen votes, the treaty was rejected, though the margin itself was only one vote.[1][3]

Senate President Jovito Salonga, who had been badly wounded in the Plaza Miranda bombing twenty years earlier, cast his vote against the treaty. The senators who voted no became known as the "Magnificent 12."[3]

## The Americans leave

The government tried to negotiate a slower withdrawal, but talks failed. Clark had already been handed over in November 1991. On 24 November 1992, the last American forces left Subic Bay, and the base was turned over to the Philippines. For the first time since 1898, there were no permanent American military bases in the country.[1][4]

## Subic and Clark after the bases

The bases were turned into special economic zones. Subic Bay became a free port, with factories, shipyards, hotels and a busy port; Clark became an industrial zone and international airport.[2][4] Thousands of former base workers had to find new jobs, and the early years were hard. Volunteers from the surrounding towns helped guard the facilities at Subic after the Americans left, so that equipment and buildings would not be looted.[2][4]

## Not the end of the story

The vote did not end military ties between the two countries. In 1999, a Visiting Forces Agreement allowed American troops to come for exercises. In 2014, a new agreement allowed them access to some Philippine bases.[4]

Supporters of the 1991 vote still see it as a proud moment of national sovereignty. Critics argue it left the Philippines weaker. Both remember it as one of the most important decisions the Philippine Senate has ever made.[3][4]
