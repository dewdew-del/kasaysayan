## Five ships looking for spice

In September 1519, five ships left Spain under the command of the Portuguese navigator Ferdinand Magellan, sailing for the Spanish king. Their goal was the Spice Islands, the Moluccas, where cloves and nutmeg grew. Those spices were worth a fortune in Europe, and Spain wanted a western route to them that did not cross Portuguese waters.[2]

The voyage was brutal. One ship was wrecked and another turned back. The crossing of the Pacific took more than three months, and sailors ate leather and sawdust to survive. In March 1521 the three remaining ships reached the islands of the Visayas.[2][4]

## An alliance in Cebu

In April the fleet sailed to Cebu, a busy trading port ruled by Rajah Humabon. Humabon received the Spaniards, traded with them and made a blood compact with Magellan. On 14 April he was baptized, together with his wife and hundreds of his people.[3]

Magellan now saw himself as the ally and protector of a Christian ruler. He demanded that the other chiefs of the area recognize Humabon's authority, and the authority of the Spanish king. Some agreed. On Mactan, a low island across a narrow channel from Cebu, a chief named Lapulapu refused.[1][3]

## Dawn on the reef

Before sunrise on 27 April 1521, Magellan crossed to Mactan with a small force. Humabon offered warriors, but Magellan is said to have told them to stay in their boats and watch.[1]

Mactan is surrounded by a wide coral reef. At low tide the ships could not come close to shore, so Magellan's men had to wade a long way through the shallows. That left them out of range of their own ships' guns.[1][3]

Pigafetta, who fought in the battle and was wounded, wrote that only about fifty men went ashore, and that they faced more than a thousand warriors, by one reading about 1,500.[2] Historians treat these numbers with care. They come from a single eyewitness on the losing side, a writer known for vivid storytelling.[1]

## The weapons of the day

The two sides fought with very different tools. Magellan's men wore helmets and breastplates and carried crossbows, muskets, swords and lances. But armor was heavy in waist-deep water, and the guns were slow to load.[1]

Lapulapu's warriors were lightly armed and moved quickly. They used long bamboo and hardwood spears, some with fire-hardened or iron tips, as well as swords and shields. They knew the reef and the tides, and they used them.[1][3]

## The death of Magellan

The fight in the water lasted about an hour. Lapulapu's warriors, armed with spears, bamboo lances and swords, targeted the men's unprotected legs. Magellan was struck several times and fell. Pigafetta's account of his captain's death, cut down in the shallows while his men retreated to the boats, is one of the most famous passages in the history of exploration.[1][3]

The survivors could not recover his body. Within days, the alliance in Cebu fell apart: at a feast hosted by Humabon, several of the expedition's officers were killed, and the remaining crews fled.[2]

## What Mactan left behind

Only one ship, the Victoria, made it back to Spain, in September 1522, completing the first voyage around the world. Of about 270 men who had set out, only 18 returned on it.[2]

Lapulapu left no writings of his own. His name appears only once in Pigafetta's account, and much about him, including his age and origins, is still debated.[2] Yet the battle made him the first remembered figure to resist foreign rule in the islands, and he is honored as a national hero.

In 2021, for the 500th anniversary, the National Quincentennial Committee chose to frame the events of 1521 around "victory and humanity": Lapulapu's victory, and the kindness of the islanders who fed and helped the starving sailors when they first arrived.[3][4] A shrine and monument stand near the shore at Punta Engaño, and the city on the island bears his name.
