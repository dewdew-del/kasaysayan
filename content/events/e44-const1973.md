## A convention under pressure

In 1971, delegates were elected to a constitutional convention to write a new charter to replace the 1935 Constitution. They argued over many questions, including whether to switch to a parliamentary system and how to stop presidents from staying in power too long.[3][4]

When martial law was declared in September 1972, the convention was still meeting. Some delegates who opposed President Marcos were arrested or went into hiding. Others came under heavy pressure. In November 1972, the convention approved a draft that kept Marcos in power during a long "transitional" period and gave him the power to issue decrees with the force of law.[3][4]

## A plebiscite cancelled

The draft was supposed to go to a vote of the people in a plebiscite. But in December 1972, Marcos postponed it. Instead, he created "citizens' assemblies," gatherings of residents in every barangay, the smallest unit of local government.[2]

## Voting by show of hands

Between 10 and 15 January 1973, these assemblies met across the country. They were asked questions such as whether they approved of the new constitution and whether they still wanted a plebiscite. In many places, people voted by raising their hands or by voice, in public, under martial law.[1][2]

The assemblies included people as young as 15 years old, below the legal voting age of the time.[2]

## "Ratified"

On 17 January 1973, Marcos issued Proclamation No. 1102, announcing that the citizens' assemblies had overwhelmingly approved the new constitution and declaring it ratified and in force.[1][2]

Under its transitional provisions, Marcos remained president with the powers of both the old presidency and the new office of prime minister, and he could rule by decree.[3]

## The Javellana case

Several citizens and senators went to the Supreme Court. In the case known as Javellana v. Executive Secretary, they argued that the constitution had not been ratified according to the law, since there had been no proper plebiscite.[1][2]

On 31 March 1973, the Court released its decision. A majority of the justices agreed that the constitution had not been ratified in the way the 1935 Constitution required. But the Court, divided, did not order it set aside. Its final ruling was that "there is no further judicial obstacle" to the new constitution being considered in force.[1][3]

Chief Justice Roberto Concepción, who had found the ratification invalid, retired soon after.[4]

## Arguments in the Court

The case was argued before justices who had themselves been asked to take an oath under the new constitution. Lawyers for the petitioners argued that the will of the people could not be measured by a show of hands taken under military rule.[2]

The government's lawyers argued that the people had accepted the new constitution, that it was already being followed, and that the Court should not interfere in a political question. In the end, the question of whether the people had truly consented was left unanswered by law.[1][2]

## The legal shield of martial law

The 1973 Constitution gave Marcos's rule a legal framework. Amendments in 1976 and 1981 increased his powers further, including the infamous Amendment No. 6, which let him issue decrees whenever he judged there was a "grave emergency."[3]

Many lawyers and historians see the Javellana decision as one of the darkest moments of the Supreme Court, the day the law gave way to power.[3][4] Others defend the justices as having had little real choice under martial law.

## Replaced in 1987

After the People Power Revolution of 1986, the 1973 Constitution was set aside. President Corazon Aquino ruled under a temporary "Freedom Constitution" until a new constitutional commission wrote the 1987 Constitution, which was approved in a real plebiscite with secret ballots.[3] Its writers, remembering 1973, added strict limits on martial law, including a 60-day limit and review by Congress and the Supreme Court.
