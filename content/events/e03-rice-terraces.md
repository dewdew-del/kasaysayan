## Stairs of green

In the mountains of the Cordillera Central, in northern Luzon, whole hillsides have been carved into steps. Stone and earth walls hold flat, flooded fields that climb from the valley floors toward the clouds. The rice terraces of Ifugao are among the best-known images of the Philippines, and several of them are listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site.

They were built and are still kept by the Ifugao people. Water from the forests at the top of the mountains is channeled down through the terraces, field by field, in a system that farmers have maintained for generations.[3]

## How the terraces work

A terrace system is only as good as its water. At the top of each mountain, families keep small private woodlots called muyong. The forest holds rainwater and releases it slowly into springs and streams. Bamboo pipes and stone channels carry it to the highest fields, and each field spills into the one below.[3]

Keeping it all working takes constant care. Walls must be rebuilt after heavy rains, channels cleared and the fields planted and harvested in a cycle tied to rituals and to the calendar of the rice. Knowledge of how to do this has been passed down within families and communities, along with the chanted epics, the hudhud, that are sung during planting and harvest.[3]

In 1995, UNESCO listed five clusters of Ifugao terraces as a World Heritage Site, recognizing them as a "living cultural landscape." That word "living" matters: the terraces survive only as long as people keep farming them.[1]

## Where "2,000 years" came from

Almost every textbook used to say the terraces were about 2,000 years old. That number goes back to the early 1900s, to the American anthropologists Roy Franklin Barton and Henry Otley Beyer.[1][2]

Neither of them had a way to date the terraces. They estimated how long it would take people to build so many fields by hand and concluded that the work must have taken many centuries. The figure was repeated so often that it became accepted as fact, including on signs and in official descriptions.[2]

## Digging for an answer

Starting in 2012, the Ifugao Archaeological Project, led by the Filipino archaeologist Stephen Acabado, began excavating at Old Kiyyangan, a village site in Kiangan long thought to be one of the first Ifugao settlements. The team worked with the Ifugao community and with the Save the Ifugao Terraces Movement.[1][2]

They collected charcoal, seeds and other remains that can be dated by radiocarbon. According to Acabado, the village was settled about 1,000 years ago, and the clear signs of wet-rice farming in the terraces appear only around 1650.[2] Earlier fields may have existed, but they seem to have been small and used for crops such as taro rather than flooded rice.[1]

## A history of resistance

If the big rice terraces really spread after 1650, that changes the story they tell. In those years Spanish colonial forces were pushing into the lowlands and trying to reach the mountains. Acabado argues that people moved into the highlands and built the terraces as a way to grow enough food to live beyond colonial control.[1][2]

In this reading, the terraces are not only a sign of ancient skill. They are also a monument to people who chose to stay free, and who built a new way of life in the mountains to do it.

## Still debated

Not everyone agrees on the new dates. Some researchers point out that radiocarbon dates only show when a particular layer formed, and that older terraces may have been rebuilt so many times that their earliest traces are gone.[3] Others note that the new results come from a limited number of sites in a very large landscape.[1]

What both sides agree on is that the 2,000-year figure was never based on evidence. The terraces' real age is a question archaeology is still answering, and the people of Ifugao are part of finding the answer. Whatever their exact age, the terraces remain a living landscape, farmed and repaired every season by the families who inherited them.
