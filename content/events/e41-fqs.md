## A second term

In November 1969, Ferdinand Marcos became the first president of the Philippines to win a second full term. His campaign spent enormous sums, and critics said the election was marked by fraud, vote-buying and violence. Soon afterward, the government faced an economic crisis: the peso lost much of its value and prices rose sharply.[2][4]

Young people were restless. Around the world, students were marching against wars, inequality and old authorities. In the Philippines, student groups had been growing for years. Some were moderate reformers. Others, influenced by new radical ideas, saw the country's problems as rooted in what they called imperialism, feudalism and bureaucrat capitalism.[3]

## The State of the Nation

On 26 January 1970, Marcos went to the Legislative Building in Manila to deliver his State of the Nation Address to Congress. Outside, thousands of students and workers gathered to protest. Moderate groups had organized the rally to demand a non-partisan constitutional convention. Radical groups joined in.[1][4]

As Marcos left the building, some protesters threw objects at his car, including, by many accounts, a cardboard coffin and a paper crocodile, symbols of death and of greed. Police charged the crowd with batons and shields. Many students were beaten and injured. The violence shocked the public.[1][2]

## The Battle of Mendiola

Four days later, on 30 January, protesters marched to Malacañang Palace. Near Mendiola Street, by the palace gates, the crowd rammed a fire truck through a gate. That night, students fought soldiers and police with stones and homemade firebombs, and government forces opened fire.[2][3]

Several students were killed. Sources give different numbers, from four to six. Hundreds were injured or arrested.[2][4] The clash became known as the Battle of Mendiola, and the street became a symbol of protest.

## Three months of marches

For the next two months, protests continued almost every week. Students marched from the University of the Philippines, the University of Santo Tomas and other schools. Labor unions, farmers' groups and jeepney drivers joined. Protesters denounced Marcos, rising prices, American influence and police brutality.[1][4]

The period, from January to March 1970, became known as the First Quarter Storm, because it fell in the first quarter of the year. The name suggested something like a typhoon sweeping through the country's politics.[1]

## Who the protesters were

The marchers came from many backgrounds. There were students from elite private universities and from public schools, young workers from factories in Manila, jeepney drivers angry about rising fuel prices, and farmers who came to the city to demand land reform.[1][4]

Their demands were different too. Moderates wanted honest elections and a fair constitutional convention. Radicals wanted a complete change of the social system. What united them was anger at the government and at the violence used against them.[3]

## A generation radicalized

The First Quarter Storm changed many of the people who took part. Some joined the reformist movements and the 1971 Constitutional Convention. Many others concluded that peaceful protest would never be enough. Some joined the New People's Army, the armed wing of the Communist Party of the Philippines, founded in 1969.[3]

For the government, the protests were proof that communists were stirring up the country. Marcos used the unrest, and the bombings and violence of the following two years, to argue that the nation was in danger.[3][4]

## Toward martial law

Historians see the First Quarter Storm as one of the steps on the road to 1972. When Marcos declared martial law in September of that year, among the first people arrested were student leaders, journalists and opposition politicians, some of whom had been part of the protests of 1970.[4]

Some of the students of 1970 later died under martial law. Others became leaders of the opposition that helped end it in 1986.[1]

## Remembering

Every January, veterans of the First Quarter Storm gather to remember the students who died. In 2020, on its fiftieth anniversary, they marched again to Mendiola.[1][2] For them, the storm of 1970 was the moment a generation learned to speak up, and paid the price for it.
