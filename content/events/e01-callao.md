## A cave above the Pinacanauan River

In the hills of Peñablanca, in Cagayan province, the Pinacanauan River cuts through limestone riddled with caves. The largest of them is Callao Cave. For a long time, few people thought of it as a place where the story of humanity might change.

Archaeologists working with the University of the Philippines, led by Armand Salvador Mijares, began digging deeper into the cave floor in the 2000s. Most of what they found was animal bone: deer, pigs and other creatures that had died or been eaten there long ago.[1]

{{photo:2}}

## One small bone

In 2007 the team recovered a single human foot bone, a third metatarsal, from a deep layer of the cave. When it was dated with the uranium-series method, it came out at about 67,000 years old. Mijares and his colleagues reported it in 2010. At the time it was the oldest direct evidence of humans in the Philippines, older than the remains from the Tabon Caves in Palawan.[1][3]

The bone was small and had unusual features, but one bone was not enough to say who its owner had been. The team went back to dig.

## Twelve more pieces

The team returned to Callao in 2011 and again in 2015. Working slowly with brushes and small tools, they sieved every bucket of soil from the deep layer where the foot bone had been. Later excavations brought up twelve more bones and teeth from the same layer: teeth, finger and toe bones, and part of a thigh bone. They belonged to at least three individuals, including a child.[1]

In April 2019 the team, led by Florent Détroit of the National Museum of Natural History in Paris together with Mijares, published their study in the journal Nature. They argued that the remains did not match any known human species. The teeth were very small, some with features seen in modern humans, while the curved finger and toe bones looked more like those of far older human relatives that still climbed trees.[1][2]

They gave the species a new name: Homo luzonensis, "human of Luzon."[1] Naming a new human species is rare. It means the scientists were confident that these people were not simply small modern humans, but belonged to a separate branch of the human family tree.

A new species built on a handful of bones from a few individuals will keep being tested as more finds come out of the ground. That kind of checking is normal in science, and it is part of why digging at sites like Callao continues.

## How old, and how did they get there?

Uranium-series dating works by measuring tiny amounts of uranium that seep into bones and teeth from groundwater after burial, and the elements that uranium slowly turns into. It gave the fossils ages of roughly 50,000 to 67,000 years. The geochemist Rainer Grün, who worked on the dating, pointed out that this method only gives a minimum age, so the bones could be older.[2]

How these people reached Luzon is still an open question. Luzon was never joined to mainland Asia by land, even when sea levels were low during the Ice Age, so their ancestors must have crossed the sea somehow, perhaps by accident on rafts of vegetation or by some deliberate crossing.[2][3] Stone tools and a butchered rhinoceros found elsewhere in Cagayan show that some early human relative was on Luzon as long as 700,000 years ago, but whether those were made by Homo luzonensis is not known.[2]

## A crowded family tree

The find put the Philippines at the center of a big scientific conversation. Like Homo floresiensis on the Indonesian island of Flores, Homo luzonensis suggests that small groups of early humans reached islands in Southeast Asia and then changed in isolation over a very long time.[2][3]

Many questions remain. Scientists have not been able to recover ancient DNA from the bones, and they still don't know when the species disappeared, or whether it ever met the modern humans who later settled the islands. What is clear is that the human story of the Philippines began far earlier, and was far stranger, than anyone had thought.
