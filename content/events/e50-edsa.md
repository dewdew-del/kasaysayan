## A coup discovered

After the disputed snap election of February 1986, a group of military officers called the Reform the Armed Forces Movement, RAM, was secretly planning a coup against President Ferdinand Marcos. On 22 February, they learned that the plot had been discovered.[1][3]

That afternoon, Defense Minister Juan Ponce Enrile, who was close to the RAM officers, and Lieutenant General Fidel V. Ramos, vice chief of staff of the armed forces, announced that they were breaking with Marcos. They barricaded themselves with a few hundred soldiers in Camp Aguinaldo and Camp Crame, two military camps facing each other across Epifanio de los Santos Avenue, EDSA, in Quezon City.[1][3]

They were badly outnumbered. If Marcos sent his loyal troops, they could not hold out.

## Cardinal Sin's call

That evening, Cardinal Jaime Sin, Archbishop of Manila, spoke on Radio Veritas, the Catholic radio station. He asked the people to go to EDSA to support the rebels and to protect them with their bodies, and to bring food.[1][3]

People came. At first a few thousand, then tens of thousands, then far more. They came in families, with children and grandparents. Priests and nuns came with rosaries and statues of the Virgin Mary. Students came, and office workers, and people from the slums.[2][3]

## Days two and three

On 23 February, the crowd grew. Ramos moved his forces to Camp Crame, and people formed a wall across EDSA as the soldiers crossed. Marcos loyalists attacked the Radio Veritas transmitter and cut it off the air, but a small station, renamed Radyo Bandido, kept broadcasting from a secret location.[2][4]

That afternoon, marines with tanks and armored vehicles approached along Ortigas Avenue. They were met by a sea of people, many kneeling and praying in the road. Nuns knelt in front of the tanks. People offered soldiers flowers, food and cigarettes. The marines stopped.[2][3]

Over the next day, more and more units of the armed forces switched sides. On 24 February, helicopter gunships sent to attack Camp Crame landed there instead, and their crews joined the rebels.[3][5]

## The fear behind the courage

The crowd's calm hid real fear. On those nights, people did not know whether soldiers would open fire, whether jets would bomb the camps, or whether their families would get home.[2][3]

## Two inaugurations

On the morning of 25 February, Corazon Aquino took her oath of office as president at Club Filipino in San Juan, with Salvador Laurel as vice president. At noon, Marcos held his own inauguration at Malacañang Palace, broadcast on the few stations still in his hands.[3][5]

## Departure

That evening, Marcos, his family and close associates left Malacañang on American helicopters. They were flown to Clark Air Base and then, the next day, to Hawaii.[5]

When the news spread, crowds celebrated in the streets all night. People entered the palace grounds. In Malacañang, they found the evidence of the family's wealth, including, famously, hundreds of pairs of Imelda Marcos's shoes.[3]

## How many came

No one counted the crowds. Estimates range from hundreds of thousands to more than a million people at the height of the four days.[3][5] What is clear is that the crowds were large enough, and peaceful enough, that soldiers sent to disperse them chose not to fire.

## A revolution argued over

People Power inspired democracy movements across the world, from Eastern Europe to South Korea. In the Philippines, it is remembered every February, at the People Power Monument and the EDSA Shrine.[5]

It is also argued over. Some historians stress the courage of ordinary people. Others stress the role of the military rebels, the Church or the United States. Some ask whether EDSA brought deep change or only replaced one group of elites with another.[3] All of those debates begin with four days in February when an unarmed crowd stood in the road and the guns did not fire.
