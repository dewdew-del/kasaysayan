## The revolution reaches the Visayas

The revolution that began near Manila in August 1896 was at first mostly a Luzon war. By late 1897 it had stalled. Under the Pact of Biak-na-Bato, Emilio Aguinaldo and other leaders agreed to stop fighting and went into exile in Hong Kong.[1][4]

In the Visayas, though, the Katipunan was still growing. In Cebu, local members had been organizing in secret, angered by the same abuses as their brothers in Luzon: heavy taxes, forced labor, friar power and the arrest of suspected rebels.[1]

{{photo:2}}

## León Kilat

Their leader was not from Cebu. Pantaleón Villegas was born in Bacong, Negros Oriental, and had worked as a laborer, a horse handler and a performer before joining the Katipunan in Manila. He became known as León Kilat, "Lightning Lion."[2]

Kilat came to Cebu in early 1898 with a commission from the revolutionary leadership to organize an uprising. He worked with local leaders, including Luis Flores, Florencio González and others, and recruited men from the towns around the city.[2][4]

## Palm Sunday

The rebels planned to strike in early April. On Sunday, 3 April 1898, Palm Sunday, they attacked. Groups of Katipuneros armed mostly with bolos and a few guns came into Cebu City from the hills and the nearby towns. Fighting broke out in the streets of the old town, in a district called Tinago and around the Spanish government buildings.[1][3]

The Spanish garrison was taken by surprise. Soldiers and Spanish residents retreated into Fort San Pedro, the old stone fort by the harbor, and into a few other strongholds. For several days, the rebels controlled most of the city.[1][4]

Estimates of how many fought vary widely, from a few hundred in the first attack to several thousand who joined over the following days.[1]

{{photo:3}}

## Four days of freedom

For about four days, from Palm Sunday to Holy Thursday, Cebu City was in the hands of León Kilat's forces. Accounts describe the rebels moving through the streets, setting fire to Spanish buildings and trying to break into the fort.[3][4]

But the rebels had few firearms and no artillery, and the walls of Fort San Pedro held. Then Spanish reinforcements arrived by sea from Iloilo, bringing more soldiers and naval guns that fired on the rebel positions.[1][3] The Katipuneros were forced to pull back out of the city into the mountains.

## Why Holy Week

The timing was deliberate. During Holy Week, many Spanish officials and soldiers would be at church services and processions, and the streets would be full of people from the countryside, so armed men coming into the city would attract less attention.[3]

It also gave the uprising a powerful meaning in a deeply Catholic island. Cebuanos later remembered the four days of freedom as falling between Palm Sunday and Good Friday, the days of Christ's entry into Jerusalem and his death.[3]

## Betrayal in Carcar

León Kilat retreated south to the town of Carcar. There, on the night of 8 April 1898, Good Friday, he was killed by men he had trusted. Accounts say he was stabbed while he slept by members of his own following, among them his aide Apolinario Alcuitas, who feared Spanish reprisals against the town.[2][3]

He was about 25 years old. His death broke the first rising in Cebu, but not the movement. Rebels kept fighting in the mountains of the island for the rest of the year.[1]

## The Spanish leave

Within months the situation changed completely. The United States and Spain went to war in April 1898, and the Spanish empire in the islands collapsed. In December 1898, after the Treaty of Paris, the Spanish garrison left Cebu, handing power to Cebuano leaders before the Americans arrived.[4]

## Remembered in Cebu

Cebu remembers the uprising every 3 April. A street in the city, and a monument, bear the name Tres de Abril, and León Kilat's statue stands in Carcar and Cebu. The revolt is a reminder that the Philippine Revolution was not only a Tagalog war: it reached the Visayas, and Visayans fought and died in it.[1][2]
