## The retreat to Bataan

After Japanese armies landed on Luzon in December 1941, General Douglas MacArthur ordered the Filipino and American forces to fall back onto the Bataan Peninsula, the mountainous arm of land on the western side of Manila Bay. The plan was to hold out there until reinforcements arrived from the United States.[2]

But the US Navy had been badly damaged at Pearl Harbor, and America's leaders had decided to focus first on defeating Germany. No reinforcements would come.[2]

## Three months of siege

From January to April 1942, the defenders of Bataan held a line across the peninsula. They fought off repeated Japanese attacks, and for a time they were one of the few Allied armies anywhere in Asia that was not retreating.[1]

The cost was terrible. There had not been enough food stored on Bataan for so many soldiers and the thousands of civilians who had fled there. Rations were cut to half, then less. Men ate horses, monkeys and anything they could find. Malaria, dysentery and beriberi spread through the camps, and there was little medicine.[1][2]

In March, President Franklin Roosevelt ordered MacArthur to leave for Australia. Before going, MacArthur promised, "I shall return."[2]

## Surrender

In early April the Japanese, reinforced, broke through the exhausted lines. On 9 April 1942, Major General Edward P. King, commanding the forces on Bataan, surrendered rather than see his starving and sick men slaughtered.[1][2]

About 76,000 soldiers became prisoners of war, roughly 64,000 Filipinos and 12,000 Americans. It was the largest surrender of an army under American command in history.[1]

## The march

The Japanese army had planned for perhaps 25,000 prisoners, not three times that number. The prisoners were ordered to walk north from Mariveles and other points on the peninsula to San Fernando, Pampanga, a distance of about 100 kilometers, in the dry-season heat.[1][3]

Many were already starving and sick. Guards beat men who fell behind or stopped to drink from roadside wells and streams. Many who could not keep up were shot or bayoneted. Filipino civilians along the road who tried to pass food or water to the prisoners risked their own lives.[1][3]

From San Fernando, the prisoners were packed into small, airless railway boxcars and taken to Capas, Tarlac, then made to walk the last kilometers to Camp O'Donnell.[1]

## The people along the road

In towns along the route, such as Orani, Lubao and San Fernando, Filipino civilians watched the long columns of prisoners pass. Many tried to help. They left water, rice balls and sugarcane by the road, or threw food to the marchers when guards looked away. Some hid escaped prisoners in their homes.[1][3]

Those who were caught helping were beaten or killed. Their courage is an important part of how Filipinos remember the march: not only as a crime against soldiers, but as a moment when ordinary people risked their lives for strangers.[4]

## How many died

No one knows exactly how many died on the march. Estimates for Filipino deaths range from about 5,000 to more than 18,000. Between about 500 and 650 Americans died.[1][3] Thousands more, mostly Filipinos, died in the following months at Camp O'Donnell from disease, hunger and mistreatment.[1]

Corregidor, the island fortress at the mouth of Manila Bay, held out a month longer. It surrendered on 6 May 1942.[2]

## After the war

General Homma, the Japanese commander in the Philippines, was tried by a US military court after the war, held responsible for the atrocities of the march, and executed in 1946.[1]

## The Day of Valor

In the Philippines, 9 April is Araw ng Kagitingan, the Day of Valor, a national holiday honoring the soldiers of Bataan and Corregidor.[4] Kilometer markers line the route of the march through Bataan and Pampanga. On Mount Samat, overlooking the battlefield, a giant cross and memorial, the Dambana ng Kagitingan, honors the defenders.[4]

For many families, Bataan is not only a story of defeat but of endurance: of soldiers who held out for months against all odds, and of ordinary people along the road who risked their lives to help.
