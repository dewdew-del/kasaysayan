## A world war reaches Manila

Between 1756 and 1763, Britain and France fought a war that spread across Europe, North America, India and the seas in between. It is called the Seven Years' War. In 1762 Spain entered on the side of France, and Britain decided to strike at Spain's empire in two places at once: Havana in the Caribbean and Manila in Asia.[1][2]

The attack on Manila was organized from British India. A fleet sailed from Madras carrying British soldiers, sailors and Indian sepoys of the East India Company.[2]

## The siege

The fleet arrived in Manila Bay in late September 1762. The city's defenders had little warning. Spain's war with Britain had begun months earlier, but the news had not yet reached the islands.[1]

The British landed south of the walls and bombarded Intramuros. Spanish soldiers, Filipino militia and others resisted for about two weeks. On 6 October 1762, the walls were breached and the city fell. British troops sacked parts of it, and the archbishop, Manuel Rojo, who was serving as acting governor, signed a surrender.[1][2]

## Anda in Bacolor

Not every Spanish official accepted the surrender. Simón de Anda y Salazar, a judge of the royal court, slipped out of the city before it fell and made his way to Bacolor, in Pampanga. There he declared himself governor and set up a Spanish government in the provinces.[1][2]

With the help of Filipino and Spanish fighters, Anda kept the British largely confined to Manila and the nearby coast. The British controlled the capital, but not the country around it.[2]

## A ransom that was never paid

As part of the surrender, the British demanded a ransom of four million Spanish dollars to spare the city from further looting. Only part of it was ever paid, and the dispute over the rest dragged on long after the war.[1][2]

The British also seized the galleon Santísima Trinidad, loaded with goods bound for Acapulco, one of the richest prizes of the war. For the merchants of Manila, whose wealth depended on the galleon trade, it was a heavy blow.[2]

## Revolts in the provinces

The fall of Manila also showed people across the islands that Spanish power could be broken. In the Ilocos, Diego Silang led a revolt in late 1762, drove the Spanish from Vigan and set up his own government. He sought British support.[1]

Silang was assassinated in 1763 by men paid by the Spanish. His widow, Gabriela Silang, took over the fight and led the rebels for several months before she was captured and executed.[1] Other uprisings broke out in Pangasinan and elsewhere. They were all eventually put down, but they left lasting memories.

## Handing the city back

The war ended with the Treaty of Paris, signed on 10 February 1763. Britain agreed to return Manila to Spain, in exchange for other concessions elsewhere in the world. The news took a long time to reach Asia.[1][2]

The British finally left Manila in early 1764. Historians give different exact dates in April and May of that year.[1] By then they had held the city for about a year and a half.

## What it left behind

The occupation was short, but it had effects. It showed Spain that its colony was vulnerable and led to reforms in defense and government. It also brought new contact with the British world: some of the Indian sepoys stayed in the islands, settling in Cainta, Rizal, where their descendants are still remembered.[3]

For Filipinos, the story of 1762 is also the story of Diego and Gabriela Silang. Gabriela is remembered as one of the first women to lead a revolt in Philippine history, and her name lives on in schools, streets and organizations.[1]

## A city between empires

The events of 1762 are a reminder that the Philippines was never isolated. Decisions made in London, Paris, Madrid and Madras could bring war to Manila Bay, and the fate of the islands could be traded across a negotiating table in Europe.[2] It would happen again in 1898.
