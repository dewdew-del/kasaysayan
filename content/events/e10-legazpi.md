## Forty-four years later

After Magellan's death at Mactan in 1521, Spain sent several more expeditions toward the islands, with little success. Then, in November 1564, a new fleet left the port of Navidad in Mexico under Miguel López de Legazpi, a royal official from the Basque country.[3]

His orders were not only to explore, but to settle. With him sailed Augustinian friars, led by Andrés de Urdaneta, a veteran navigator. The plan was to find a way to sail back across the Pacific to Mexico, something no ship had yet managed, so that a lasting link between the islands and the Spanish empire could be built.[3]

## A town that would not meet them

The fleet reached the Visayas early in 1565 and, in April, arrived at Cebu, the port where Magellan had been welcomed and then turned away.[3]

Legazpi sent word to the ruler of Cebu, Rajah Tupas, asking to meet and promising peaceful intentions. Tupas, who remembered what had happened in 1521, did not come, despite several attempts. Some accounts say he moved his people into the interior of the island.[1][3]

Legazpi decided to use force. On 27 April 1565, Spanish ships fired on the town and soldiers landed. Houses were burned, and the people fled.[1][3]

## A box in a burned house

The next day, 28 April, a sailor named Juan de Camus was searching the ruins. In a house that had escaped the worst of the fire, or in its ashes, depending on the account, he found a pine box. Inside was a small wooden image of the Child Jesus, dressed in red, with a cross and globe.[1][2]

The Spaniards recognized it as the image that Magellan had given to Humabon's wife at her baptism 44 years earlier. It had been kept and honored by the people of Cebu all that time.[2][4] The friars saw the discovery as a sign of divine favor. The finding is remembered in Cebu as the Kaplag, from the Cebuano word for "found," and is celebrated every year.[1]

{{photo:3}}

## Why the image survived

How the image survived for 44 years is part of its story. After Magellan's death the people of Cebu had no Spanish priests and little contact with Christianity. Yet they seem to have kept the image as an object of power and honor, treating it with care in their own way.[2][4]

Accounts from the time say that the people of Cebu used it in rituals to ask for rain. Whatever its meaning to them, they did not destroy it, and it was still there, wrapped and boxed, when the Spaniards returned.[1][4]

## A treaty and a fort

Over the following weeks, Legazpi made peace with Tupas. A treaty signed in mid-1565 placed Cebu under Spanish protection, with the people paying tribute in return.[3] The Spaniards built a settlement they named San Miguel, later the Villa del Santísimo Nombre de Jesús, the first Spanish town in the Philippines, along with a wooden fort that would later be rebuilt in stone as Fort San Pedro.[3]

On 1 June 1565, one of Legazpi's ships, the San Pedro, left Cebu with Urdaneta on board. By sailing north to catch westerly winds, it reached Mexico months later, discovering the route that would carry the Manila galleons for the next 250 years.[3]

## The start of Spanish rule

From Cebu, Legazpi's forces spread to Panay and then north to Luzon, where in 1571 he founded the Spanish city of Manila.[3] More than three centuries of Spanish rule followed.

For Cebu, 1565 is remembered in two ways. It was a conquest, carried out with guns and fire. It was also the moment the Santo Niño was found, an image that grew into the most beloved religious figure in the Visayas and the center of the Sinulog festival. Both memories are part of the city's history.[1][4]

The image is kept today at the Basilica Minore del Santo Niño, built near the place where it was found. It is one of the oldest Christian objects in the Philippines.[1]
