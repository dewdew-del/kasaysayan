## A town on the coast of Samar

Balangiga is a small fishing and farming town on the southern coast of Samar, facing the Leyte Gulf. In August 1901, a company of American soldiers, Company C of the 9th US Infantry, was stationed there to stop supplies from reaching Filipino guerrillas led by General Vicente Lukbán.[1]

At first relations were calm. But the soldiers' presence became harsh. They forced the town's men to cut brush and clear land, confiscated food and, according to Filipino accounts, mistreated residents, including women. Anger grew.[1][4]

## The plan

The town's leaders, working with the guerrillas, prepared a surprise. In the weeks before the attack, men from the surrounding area came into town, some disguised as laborers. On the night before, some of them reportedly dressed as women and carried bolos hidden in small coffins, saying they were taking dead children to the church.[1]

The signal would be the church bells.

## Sunday breakfast

Early on Sunday, 28 September 1901, while most of the American soldiers were eating breakfast without their rifles, a local police chief grabbed a sentry's gun and the church bells began to ring. Hundreds of attackers armed with bolos rushed the soldiers from the church, the town hall and the nearby houses.[1][2]

The fight was short and brutal. Of about 74 Americans in the company, roughly 48 were killed and most of the rest wounded. A few escaped by boat along the coast to the next American garrison.[1][3] It was one of the worst single defeats of the US Army in the war.

## "A howling wilderness"

The American response was terrible. General Jacob H. Smith, sent to command on Samar, ordered his troops to turn the island into "a howling wilderness." He was reported to have ordered them to kill everyone over the age of ten who was capable of bearing arms.[1][4]

Over the following months, American troops burned towns, destroyed crops and food supplies and killed large numbers of Filipinos across Samar. Estimates of the dead vary enormously, from about 2,500 to tens of thousands.[1][4] Smith was later court-martialed in the United States, but his punishment was only to be retired from the army.[1]

## A war on the whole island

The campaign on Samar became a scandal in the United States. Reports of the burning of towns and the killing of civilians reached American newspapers, and the US Senate held hearings on American conduct in the Philippines in 1902.[1]

Officers who had carried out Smith's orders, including Major Littleton Waller of the Marines, were also put on trial. The proceedings revealed how harshly the war in the islands was being fought, and fed a growing anti-imperialist movement in America.[1][4]

## Taken as trophies

When American troops left Balangiga, they took the town's three church bells, the bells that had rung the signal for the attack. Two were displayed for decades at an Air Force base in Cheyenne, Wyoming, as a memorial to the dead soldiers. The third went with the 9th Infantry and ended up at a US base in South Korea.[2][3]

For the people of Balangiga and for many Filipinos, the bells were not trophies but stolen property and a symbol of an unhealed wound. For more than a century, Filipino groups, church leaders, presidents and some American veterans asked for them to be returned.[2][4]

## Coming home

In 2018, after the US Congress lifted legal obstacles, the American government agreed to return them. On 11 December 2018 the three bells arrived in Manila, and on 15 December they were brought back to the church in Balangiga, where crowds wept and cheered.[2][3]

## Two kinds of memory

In the United States, the attack was long remembered as the "Balangiga massacre," a tragedy for the soldiers who died. In the Philippines it is remembered as an act of resistance against a foreign army, followed by a far greater massacre of Filipinos.[1]

Both memories describe real suffering. The return of the bells did not erase that history, but it allowed the two countries to remember it together.
