## Japan's last gamble

When American troops landed on Leyte on 20 October 1944, Japan's leaders knew that losing the Philippines would cut them off from the oil and resources of Southeast Asia. Without those, their navy and war machine could not keep going.[1]

So they decided to risk almost everything. Japan's navy, the Combined Fleet, would attack the American ships off Leyte from several directions at once and destroy the landing force before it could be reinforced.[1][2]

{{photo:2}}

## A plan in four parts

The plan was complicated. A powerful force of battleships under Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita would cross the Sibuyan Sea in the central Philippines, pass through the San Bernardino Strait between Luzon and Samar, and attack Leyte Gulf from the north.[1]

A smaller southern force would come through the Surigao Strait between Leyte and Mindanao. Meanwhile, a group of Japanese aircraft carriers, which had lost most of their planes, would sail toward the main American fleet from the north as bait, to draw the American battleships away.[1][2]

## Four battles in four days

What followed was not one battle but four, fought from 23 to 26 October across hundreds of kilometers of the Philippine seas.[1][2]

In the Sibuyan Sea, American carrier planes attacked Kurita's force. They sank the giant battleship Musashi, one of the two largest warships ever built, after hitting it with many bombs and torpedoes.[1]

In the Surigao Strait, on the night of 24–25 October, American and Australian ships waiting in the dark destroyed the southern Japanese force almost completely. It was the last battle in history in which battleships fought each other.[2][3]

Off Cape Engaño, north of Luzon, American carriers sank the Japanese decoy carriers.[1]

{{photo:3}}

## The fight off Samar

The most desperate fight came off Samar on the morning of 25 October. The bait had worked: the main American battleships had gone north. Kurita's battleships came through the San Bernardino Strait and found only a small group of escort carriers and destroyers off the coast of Samar.[1][2]

The American destroyers and destroyer escorts charged the Japanese battleships, laying smoke screens and firing torpedoes, while planes from the small carriers attacked again and again. Several American ships were sunk. Confused and believing he faced a much bigger force, Kurita turned back.[1][3]

## Why Kurita turned back

Admiral Kurita's decision to withdraw off Samar, when the American landing ships in Leyte Gulf lay almost undefended ahead of him, has been debated by historians ever since.[1]

His force had been under attack for days, he had lost ships and men, and smoke, rain and the fury of the American attacks made it hard to see what he faced. He may also have believed, wrongly, that he was fighting the main American carrier fleet. Whatever the reason, the landing force was saved.[1][3]

## The first kamikazes

The battle also saw something new and terrible. On 25 October, Japanese pilots began deliberately crashing their planes into American ships, sinking the escort carrier St. Lo. These were the first organized kamikaze attacks of the war, and the tactic would be used until the war ended.[1][2]

## The end of a navy

By the end of the battle, Japan had lost several battleships, aircraft carriers, cruisers and destroyers, and thousands of sailors. More than 200,000 sailors and airmen took part on both sides, which is why Leyte Gulf is often called the largest naval battle in history.[1][3]

Japan's navy never again fought a major battle. The American landing on Leyte was safe, and the liberation of the Philippines could go on.[2]

## Seas that remember

The battle was fought in waters that Filipinos fish and sail every day: the Sibuyan Sea, the Surigao Strait, the coast of Samar. Wrecks of the ships still lie on the seabed. In 2015, explorers found the wreck of the Musashi deep in the Sibuyan Sea, more than seventy years after it sank.[2]
