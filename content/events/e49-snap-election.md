## Calling an election

By late 1985, President Ferdinand Marcos was under heavy pressure. The economy was in crisis, the communist insurgency was growing, and since the assassination of Ninoy Aquino, protests had filled the streets. In the United States, his long-time ally, many officials were questioning his support.[1]

In November 1985, in an interview on American television, Marcos announced a "snap" presidential election, early and with little notice, to prove that he still had the people's support. It was set for 7 February 1986.[1][4]

## Cory

The opposition needed one candidate. Many leaders wanted to run, but the person with the most support was Corazon "Cory" Aquino, Ninoy's widow, a quiet woman who had never held office.[1]

After a petition with a million signatures urged her to run, she agreed. Salvador "Doy" Laurel, another opposition leader, gave up his own ambition and became her running mate. Her campaign color was yellow, and its gesture was the "L" sign, for laban, "fight."[1]

Marcos mocked her inexperience, saying she was only a housewife. She answered that she had no experience in cheating, stealing, lying or killing opponents.[1]

## Guarding the ballots

On election day, 7 February 1986, millions of Filipinos voted. Volunteers from the National Citizens' Movement for Free Elections, NAMFREL, and from parishes across the country guarded polling places and ballot boxes. Many reported vote-buying, intimidation, missing names on the voter lists and ballot boxes being stolen.[1][4] Some volunteers were attacked, and some were killed.

As votes were counted, NAMFREL's quick count showed Aquino ahead. The official count by the Commission on Elections, COMELEC, showed Marcos ahead.[1][4]

## The walkout

On the night of 9 February, at the COMELEC's tabulation center in Manila, 35 computer workers who were entering the official results stood up and walked out. They said the numbers they were being told to post did not match the returns, and that the count was being manipulated.[2][3]

The tabulators, mostly young women, took shelter in Baclaran Church, afraid for their safety.[2] Their walkout was covered by foreign journalists and became one of the most famous moments of the election.

## A killing in Antique

On 11 February, Evelio Javier, a former governor of Antique and an Aquino campaign leader, was shot dead in front of the provincial capitol in San José, Antique, where votes were being counted.[1][4] His death enraged the opposition and the Church.

## The world was watching

Hundreds of foreign journalists and election observers had come to cover the vote. An American delegation led by Senator Richard Lugar reported widespread fraud and intimidation. Television pictures of volunteers protecting ballot boxes and of the tabulators' walkout were shown around the world.[1][4]

That international attention made it harder for the government to simply declare victory and move on. In the United States, President Ronald Reagan, a long-time friend of Marcos, at first suggested that both sides had cheated, then came under heavy criticism.[1]

## Two winners

On 13 February, the Catholic Bishops' Conference of the Philippines issued a statement saying the election had been marred by fraud unparalleled in the country's history and that a government that came to power that way had no moral basis.[1][4]

On 15 February, the Batasang Pambansa, controlled by Marcos's party, proclaimed him the winner, with about 53.6 percent of the vote to Aquino's 46.1 percent.[1][4]

The next day, Aquino held a huge rally at the Luneta. She claimed victory and called for a campaign of civil disobedience: boycotts of businesses owned by Marcos's allies, and a refusal to accept his government.[1]

## Days away

Few people realized how close the end was. Within a week, a group of military officers who had been planning a coup were discovered, and their leaders took refuge in two military camps on EDSA. What happened next would be called People Power.[1][3]
