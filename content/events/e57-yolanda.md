## A storm across the Pacific

In early November 2013, a typhoon formed in the western Pacific and grew stronger and stronger as it moved toward the Philippines. Its international name was Haiyan. The Philippine weather agency, PAGASA, named it Yolanda.[1]

Forecasters around the world watched it with alarm. By the time it neared land, satellite measurements suggested it was one of the strongest tropical cyclones ever observed, with sustained winds well over 300 kilometers per hour by some estimates.[1][2]

{{photo:2}}

## Warnings

PAGASA raised its highest storm signals and warned of storm surges along the coasts of Samar and Leyte. Hundreds of thousands of people were evacuated.[4]

But many did not understand what a "storm surge" meant. The term was unfamiliar to many people living along the coast. Some thought of it as high waves, not as the sea itself rising several meters and rushing inland. Some evacuation centers were built in places the surge would reach.[4]

## Landfall

Before dawn on 8 November 2013, at about 4:40 in the morning local time, Yolanda made its first landfall at Guiuan, at the southern tip of Eastern Samar. The town was almost flattened.[2][3]

The storm then crossed Leyte Gulf and swept across Leyte, Cebu, Panay and other islands of the Visayas, making several more landfalls through the day.[1]

{{photo:3}}

## The sea comes in

In Tacloban, the capital of Leyte, the worst damage came from water. The storm pushed the sea up into Leyte Gulf and onto the low-lying city. A surge of about five to six meters rushed inland, in some places up to two kilometers, within minutes.[4]

It swept away houses, cars, ships and people. Even strong buildings were flooded to their upper floors. Many of those who died on Leyte drowned in the surge.[1][4]

## The aftermath

When the storm passed, Tacloban, Guiuan and many towns across the Eastern Visayas were in ruins. Power, water and phone service were gone. Roads were blocked by debris, and the airport was badly damaged. Survivors searched for food, water and missing family members.[1][3]

The government's disaster council confirmed about 6,300 deaths across the country, most of them in the Eastern Visayas, with more than a thousand people missing. Some estimates are higher. Millions of people lost their homes or livelihoods.[1]

## Survivors' stories

Survivors told of clinging to roofs and floating debris as the water rose, of children torn from their parents' arms, and of whole neighborhoods disappearing in minutes. In Tacloban, people who had sheltered in the city's convention center and in schools near the shore found the sea coming in through the doors and windows.[1][4]

In the days after, many walked for hours to reach relatives, searched among the ruins for the missing, and waited at the airport for a seat on a military plane out of the city.[1][3]

## Help from everywhere

Help came from across the Philippines and from around the world: soldiers, doctors, volunteers, ships, planes and aid groups from dozens of countries. Filipinos in other provinces packed relief goods, and overseas Filipinos sent money home.[1]

The relief effort was one of the largest in the country's history. It was also criticized for delays and poor coordination in the first days, when many survivors waited a long time for food and water.[1]

## Lessons

Yolanda changed how the Philippines prepares for disasters. Warnings now describe storm surges in plain words, sometimes in local languages, such as how high the water may reach and how far inland. Hazard maps were redrawn, and evacuation planning and building rules were reviewed.[4]

Rebuilding took years and was uneven. Many families were moved to new housing sites far from the coast and their old jobs. Each November, survivors in Tacloban, Guiuan and the other towns hit by the storm light candles at mass graves and memorials for the people they lost.[1]
