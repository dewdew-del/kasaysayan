## Priests without parishes

In the 1800s, the richest parishes in the Philippines were held by Spanish friars of the religious orders. Filipino secular priests, trained and ordained in the islands, were usually given only the poorer ones, or served as assistants. Many of them pushed for the right to lead parishes of their own, a cause known as secularization.[2][3]

Leading voices in the movement included Father José Burgos, a young scholar from Vigan with degrees in theology and law, and two older priests in Manila and Cavite: Father Mariano Gómez, the parish priest of Bacoor, and Father Jacinto Zamora.[3]

To the friars, the secularization campaign was a threat to their power. To the colonial government, which relied on the friars to run the towns, it looked dangerous too.[2]

## The night at the arsenal

On 20 January 1872, about two hundred Filipino soldiers and workers at the Spanish arsenal in Cavite rose in revolt. They had just lost their exemption from paying tribute and doing forced labor. They killed several Spanish officers, but the uprising was crushed within days.[2]

What the mutiny really was is still debated. Governor-General Rafael de Izquierdo and other Spanish officials described it as the start of a planned revolution. Filipino accounts, especially that of the scholar Trinidad Pardo de Tavera, described it as a local protest by angry workers that the authorities seized on to strike at the reformers they already feared.[2]

## Why the government struck so hard

The colonial government in 1872 was nervous. In Spain, a revolution in 1868 had brought a period of liberal reform, and some of those ideas had reached Manila, where Filipino priests, lawyers and students had begun to speak openly about equality. A new, conservative governor-general, Izquierdo, had arrived determined to reverse that trend.[2]

For him, a mutiny in Cavite was proof that reform talk led to rebellion. It gave him the reason he needed to arrest the people he saw as troublemakers, whether or not they had anything to do with the arsenal.[2]

## A trial without proof

In the weeks that followed, the government arrested many priests, lawyers and businessmen associated with reform. Some were exiled to the Marianas. Gómez, Burgos and Zamora were tried by a military court, accused of being the leaders behind the mutiny.[2][3]

The evidence against them was weak. Accounts of the trial describe testimony from a witness who had been promised a pardon. On 15 February 1872 the court sentenced all three to death.[2] The Archbishop of Manila, Gregorio Meliton Martínez, refused to strip them of their priesthood, saying he did not believe they were guilty.[2]

## Bagumbayan

On the morning of 17 February 1872, the three priests were executed by garrote, an iron collar tightened around the neck, on the field of Bagumbayan, outside the walls of Manila. A large crowd watched. Many people knelt and prayed, and some said the church bells of Manila rang for the dead.[2][3]

Gómez was about 72 years old. Burgos was 35, and Zamora 36.[2]

## A spark

The executions shocked many Filipinos, especially educated young people who had not thought of themselves as enemies of Spain. One of them was a ten-year-old boy from Calamba named José Rizal, whose older brother Paciano had been a student and friend of Burgos.[3]

Nineteen years later, Rizal dedicated his second novel, El Filibusterismo, to the three priests, writing that the Church's refusal to defrock them showed their innocence.[2][3] Historians widely see their deaths as one of the first sparks of a Filipino national consciousness that led to the revolution of 1896.[1]

## Remembered as one

The three priests are remembered together as GomBurZa, from the first letters of their names. Each year on 17 February, the National Historical Commission of the Philippines leads a commemoration at the site of their execution, near today's Rizal Park.[1] The movement they died for also succeeded in the end: in the 20th century, Filipino priests and bishops came to lead the Church in the Philippines.
