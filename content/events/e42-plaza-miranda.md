## The opposition's rally

In August 1971, the Philippines was preparing for midterm elections. The opposition Liberal Party was campaigning hard against President Ferdinand Marcos's Nacionalista Party, and its senatorial candidates were drawing big crowds.[1][3]

On the night of 21 August, the Liberals held their final rally to proclaim their candidates at Plaza Miranda, the square in front of Quiapo Church in the heart of old Manila. Plaza Miranda was the traditional place for political rallies, sometimes called the country's "freedom park." Thousands of people filled it.[1][2]

{{photo:2}}

## Two grenades

At about 9:13 p.m., as the candidates were being presented on the stage, two fragmentation grenades were thrown onto the stage from the crowd.[1][3]

The explosions tore through the platform. Nine people were killed, among them a photographer and a five-year-old child. Dozens were wounded; sources give figures from about 60 to 95. Almost every Liberal Party senatorial candidate on stage was hurt, some very badly. Senator Jovito Salonga was left nearly blind and deaf in one ear. Senator Sergio Osmeña Jr. was severely wounded.[1][3][4]

One leading Liberal, Senator Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr., was not on the stage. He had arrived late.[1]

## Chaos in the square

In the moments after the blasts, the plaza was filled with smoke, screaming and confusion. People ran in every direction. Others rushed to the stage to pull the wounded from the wreckage of the platform.[1][2]

Injured candidates and supporters were taken to hospitals around Manila. Doctors worked through the night. Some of the wounded would carry shrapnel in their bodies for the rest of their lives.[1]

The news spread fast on radio. For many Filipinos, the idea that someone would throw grenades at a crowd of voters and candidates in the most famous square in the capital was almost unthinkable.[2]

{{photo:3}}

## Habeas corpus suspended

Hours later, President Marcos blamed communists for the attack. He suspended the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, the right of a person under arrest to be brought before a court. This allowed people to be arrested and held without charges, and many activists were detained.[3][4]

The suspension was a warning of what was to come. A year later, Marcos would declare martial law.

## The voters answer

The bombing seems to have hurt Marcos's party at the polls. In the November 1971 elections, Liberal candidates, many of them still recovering from their injuries, won most of the Senate seats being contested.[1][3]

## Who did it?

More than fifty years later, no one has been convicted for the Plaza Miranda bombing.[4]

At the time, many in the opposition suspected Marcos himself, believing the attack was meant to create fear and justify a crackdown. Others accepted the government's claim that communists were responsible.[2][4]

In the late 1980s, some former members of the Communist Party of the Philippines said publicly that party leaders had ordered the attack, hoping it would provoke Marcos into repression that would push people toward revolution. The party's founder, José María Sison, denied this.[3] Neither side has produced proof that settles the question.[4]

## Why it still matters

The unsolved bombing has cast a long shadow. For those who believe Marcos was behind it, it is evidence of how far he was willing to go to keep power. For those who believe the communists did it, it is evidence of how far they were willing to go to start a revolution.[3][4]

Historians have cautioned against certainty in either direction without new evidence. What is clear is that the attack, and the government's response to it, pushed the country further toward the crisis of 1972.[2]

## A plaza remembered

For many Filipinos, the bombing marked the end of an era of open, rowdy, democratic campaigning. Within a year, martial law closed Congress and the newspapers, and rallies like the one at Plaza Miranda became impossible.[2]

Today, Plaza Miranda is again a busy public square, crowded with devotees of the Black Nazarene, vendors and passers-by. A marker remembers the victims. Each August, survivors and members of the Liberal Party mark the anniversary.[2][4] The date, 21 August, would become even more famous twelve years later, when Ninoy Aquino was assassinated on the same day.[1]
