## A country on edge

By 1972, Ferdinand Marcos was in his second and, under the 1935 Constitution, last term as president. A constitutional convention was writing a new charter, and there were rumors that he wanted to stay in power.[1][4]

The country was tense. Student protests had continued since the First Quarter Storm of 1970. The Plaza Miranda bombing of 1971 had shocked the nation. Through 1972, a series of bombings hit Manila. The government blamed communists and pointed to the growing New People's Army. Opposition leaders such as Senator Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr. warned in speeches that Marcos was planning to seize power.[1][3]

## The ambush

On the night of 22 September 1972, the government reported that Defense Secretary Juan Ponce Enrile's car had been ambushed and riddled with bullets in Wack-Wack, Mandaluyong. Enrile was not hurt; he was said to have been riding in another car.[2]

The ambush was presented as one more sign that the country was under attack. Marcos wrote in his diary that it was "the last straw." In 1986, Enrile said the ambush had been staged. In his 2012 memoir, he said it had been real.[2]

## Arrests in the night

That same night, before any public announcement, soldiers began arresting people. Among the first were Ninoy Aquino and Senator José W. Diokno. By dawn, about a hundred of some four hundred people on the government's list had been taken to Camp Crame.[1][2]

Military units took over newspapers, radio and television stations. Telephone lines were cut. People in Manila woke up on Saturday, 23 September, to find no newspapers and most stations off the air.[1][3]

## The announcement

At about 3 p.m. on 23 September, Press Secretary Francisco Tatad read Proclamation No. 1081 on the only stations still broadcasting. That evening Marcos himself appeared on television to explain it.[1][2]

The proclamation was dated 21 September 1972. It placed the entire Philippines under martial law, citing a communist rebellion, "lawless violence" and a threat to the Republic. When exactly it was signed is still debated.[1]

## Rule by decree

Congress was closed. Marcos began to govern by presidential decree. A curfew was imposed, and public gatherings, strikes and rallies were banned. Many newspapers and broadcast stations were shut down; the ones allowed to reopen were owned by Marcos's allies.[3][4]

In the following months and years, thousands of people were detained without charges: activists, students, journalists, labor leaders, priests and opposition politicians.[4]

## A long list of names

The people arrested in the first days included senators, congressmen, journalists, publishers, student leaders and some delegates to the constitutional convention. Many were held at Camp Crame and other military camps, some for years, without trial.[1][3]

Some were released after signing pledges of loyalty. Others, like Ninoy Aquino, stayed in detention for years. Some activists went underground or into exile abroad. Families often did not know for days where their relatives had been taken.[3]

## Supporters and critics

At first, some Filipinos welcomed martial law. Streets seemed safer, private armies were disarmed and government projects moved quickly. Marcos promised a "New Society" of discipline and development, and many roads, bridges and buildings were built in the years that followed.[4]

Critics saw something else: the end of democracy, the silencing of the press, corruption by those close to the president, rising foreign debt and serious human rights abuses.[3][4]

## Fourteen years

Martial law was formally lifted in 1981, but Marcos kept most of his powers until he was removed by the People Power Revolution in February 1986.[4]

Human rights groups later documented thousands of cases of detention, torture, disappearance and killing during those years. In 2013, Republic Act No. 10368 recognized the victims and set up a board to compensate them.[4] How martial law is remembered remains one of the most debated questions in the Philippines today.
