## The way back

Sailing west across the Pacific from Mexico to Asia was hard but possible: the trade winds carried ships toward the islands. The real problem was getting back. For decades after Magellan, no ship had found a way to cross the Pacific from Asia to the Americas.[1]

In 1565 that changed. A ship from Miguel López de Legazpi's expedition, guided by the Augustinian friar and navigator Andrés de Urdaneta, sailed north from the Philippines until it caught the westerly winds and the Kuroshio current, then rode them east to the coast of California and down to Mexico. The Spanish called the return route the tornaviaje.[1][3]

With a way there and a way back, a regular link between the Philippines and Spain's American empire became possible. It would last 250 years.

## Silk for silver

The trade that grew up on this route was simple in idea and enormous in scale. Chinese junks, many from Fujian, brought silk, porcelain, lacquerware, ivory and other luxury goods to Manila. Spanish merchants bought them, mostly with silver, and loaded them onto galleons bound for Acapulco.[1]

In Mexico, the goods were sold at a great fair. Some went on to Peru, some across Mexico by mule and then on to Spain. The galleons came back loaded with Mexican and Peruvian silver, along with soldiers, officials, priests, letters and new arrivals to the colony.[1][2]

China, whose economy ran on silver, absorbed huge amounts of it. Historians often describe the galleon trade as the moment when the world economy became truly global, with Manila as one of its hinges.[3]

## The voyage

Galleons usually left Cavite, the port of Manila, at the end of June or in early July, to catch the summer winds. The eastward crossing to Acapulco could take four to six months. The return trip west, leaving Mexico around March or April, was shorter.[1]

The ships were among the largest of their day. Many were built in the Philippines, at shipyards in Cavite and elsewhere, using hardwoods cut from local forests and labor drawn from Filipino communities.[2] For the men who cut the timber and built the ships, often under forced-labor rules, the trade meant hard work far from home.

## Shipwrecks and sailors

The crossing was dangerous. Storms, typhoons, scurvy and overloading sank many ships. One count lists about 108 voyages, with 26 galleons lost at sea, though historians give different totals.[1] Wrecks off the Philippines, Guam and the coast of California are still being found and studied.

Filipino sailors and craftsmen worked aboard the galleons. Some stayed in Mexico, settling around Acapulco and the Pacific coast, where words, foods and customs from the islands mixed with local ones. Mexican influences traveled the other way too: words in Tagalog and other languages, foods such as maize and chili, and religious images all came on the ships.[2]

## The last galleon

The trade slowed in the early 1800s as Spain's empire weakened. In 1815, during Mexico's war of independence, the route came to an end, and the last galleon crossed the Pacific.[1] Mexico won its independence in 1821.

## What it left behind

The galleon trade made Manila one of the great trading cities of Asia, home to Spanish, Chinese, Filipino, Japanese and mixed communities. It also shaped the colony: the Spanish government in the islands depended for centuries on the silver subsidy that came on the ships, and many settlers cared more about the annual galleon than about developing the islands.[1][3]

Today the trade is remembered in museums and festivals on both sides of the Pacific. Commemorations of the galleon trade in both countries are a reminder that the two countries were once linked by the same ships for two and a half centuries.[2]
