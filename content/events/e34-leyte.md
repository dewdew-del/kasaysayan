## "I shall return"

When General Douglas MacArthur left Corregidor for Australia in March 1942, he made a promise: "I shall return." Over the next two and a half years, as American forces fought their way across the Pacific, those words were repeated in leaflets dropped over the islands, on radios hidden from the Japanese, and in the songs and hopes of Filipinos living under occupation.[3]

In the Philippines, the war never fully stopped. Guerrilla groups across the islands kept fighting, gathered intelligence and sent radio reports to MacArthur's headquarters. On Leyte, guerrillas led by Colonel Ruperto Kangleon controlled much of the countryside.[3]

## Why Leyte

By the middle of 1944, American planners had to decide where to strike. Some wanted to bypass the Philippines and attack Formosa. MacArthur argued that America had a moral duty to free the Filipinos first, and he won the argument.[3]

Leyte, in the eastern Visayas, was chosen for the first landings. It had deep water close to shore, it lay in the middle of the archipelago, and taking it would split the Japanese forces in the north and south.[1][3]

## A-Day

On 20 October 1944, after heavy bombardment from warships offshore, American troops of the Sixth Army landed on beaches along Leyte's east coast, at Palo, Tacloban and Dulag. More than a hundred thousand soldiers came ashore in the first days.[1][3]

That afternoon, MacArthur came in on a landing craft to Red Beach, near Palo. The boat ran aground in shallow water, and he waded the last stretch to shore. With him were President Sergio Osmeña, who had succeeded Manuel Quezon after Quezon's death in August, and General Carlos P. Romulo. The scene was photographed and filmed, and the pictures were seen around the world.[1][2]

## "I have returned"

Standing near the beach, in the rain, MacArthur spoke into a radio microphone. "People of the Philippines," he said, "I have returned. By the grace of Almighty God our forces stand again on Philippine soil." He called on Filipinos to rise and strike at the enemy.[2][3]

Osmeña spoke too, announcing that the Commonwealth government was back on Philippine soil. Days later, in Tacloban, he formally restored the Commonwealth government, making the town the temporary capital of the Philippines.[1][4]

## Waiting on the island

For the people of Leyte, the landing came after more than two years of occupation. Food had been scarce, Japanese soldiers had taken rice and livestock, and suspected guerrillas and their families had been arrested or killed.[3]

When the bombardment began on the morning of 20 October, many families fled inland from the coast. Some watched from the hills as the sea filled with ships, more than most had ever imagined existed. For them, the sight was both frightening and a sign that the occupation might finally end.[1][4]

## The fight for Leyte

The landing was only the beginning. Japan rushed reinforcements to Leyte and sent nearly its whole remaining navy to attack the American fleet, leading to the Battle of Leyte Gulf from 23 to 26 October.[3]

On land, the fighting lasted until the end of 1944, in rain, mud and mountains. Filipino guerrillas guided American troops, scouted Japanese positions and fought alongside them.[3] Leyte's towns and people suffered heavily.

## The road to Manila

From Leyte, American and Filipino forces moved on. They landed on Mindoro in December 1944 and at Lingayen Gulf on Luzon in January 1945, and fought their way to Manila. The liberation of the islands would take most of 1945.[3]

## The memorial at Red Beach

Today, at Red Beach in Palo, a larger-than-life bronze statue shows MacArthur, Osmeña, Romulo and others wading through a pool of water, re-creating the famous photograph. Every 20 October, Leyte marks the anniversary, sometimes with veterans of the war and visitors from the United States.[4]

For Leyte, the landing is a point of pride: the place where the liberation of the Philippines began.[1]
