## From war to politics

By 1907, organized fighting against American rule had mostly ended in the lowlands of Luzon and the Visayas, though resistance went on in some regions. The American colonial government, led by a civilian Philippine Commission, now wanted to show that it could govern with Filipino cooperation.[1]

The US Congress had passed the Philippine Organic Act in 1902. It promised that once peace was established and a census taken, Filipinos would be able to elect a lower house of a legislature. The census was completed in 1905, and the way was open.[1][2]

{{photo:2}}

## The first election

On 30 July 1907, Filipino voters went to the polls to elect 80 members of the new Philippine Assembly. Voting was limited to men who were literate in English or Spanish, owned property or paid a certain amount in taxes, or had held public office. Only a small share of the population could vote.[1][2]

The winner was the Nacionalista Party, which campaigned for "immediate independence." It took a clear majority of the seats. The party that had supported American rule, the Progresistas, came a distant second.[1]

The result surprised many Americans. Filipino voters had used their first election to say, clearly, that they still wanted their own country.

## Opening night

On 16 October 1907, the Philippine Assembly held its first session at the Manila Grand Opera House on Plaza Cervantes. There was no building yet big enough or grand enough, so the legislature met in a theater.[2]

The American secretary of war, William Howard Taft, who had been the first civilian governor of the islands, came to Manila for the occasion and gave a speech.[1]

## Osmeña and Quezon

The Assembly elected as its speaker Sergio Osmeña, a 29-year-old lawyer and journalist from Cebu who led the Nacionalistas. His close ally, Manuel L. Quezon of Tayabas, became the majority floor leader.[1][3]

Osmeña would serve as speaker until 1922. For the next four decades, he and Quezon, sometimes partners and sometimes rivals, would dominate Filipino politics.[3]

## Who sat in the Assembly

Most members of the first Assembly came from the same small elite: lawyers, landowners, merchants and provincial officials, many educated in Manila or abroad. Several had fought in the revolution against Spain or in the war against the Americans, and now chose to continue the fight for independence through politics.[1]

They were young. Osmeña was 29 and Quezon 29, and many members were in their thirties. Within a few years they had learned the rules of the colonial system well enough to use them against it.[3]

## How much power?

The Assembly was only half of the legislature. The upper house was the Philippine Commission itself, made up mostly of Americans appointed by the US president. Laws needed the approval of both houses, and the American governor-general could veto them.[1][2]

So Filipinos could debate, propose and block laws, but Americans still had the final word. Even so, the Assembly was the first elected national legislature in Asia, and it gave Filipino leaders a public stage and a training ground in government.[2]

## A legislature of their own

The Assembly also gave Filipinos a way to press for independence through politics instead of war. In 1916, the Jones Law replaced the Commission with an elected Philippine Senate, so that both houses were Filipino. It promised independence once a "stable government" was established.[1]

Among the Assembly's first acts were laws on public schools, roads and local government, along with resolutions asking the United States for independence. Debates were held in Spanish, the language of most of the educated members, even as English spread in the new public schools.[2]

## Where it led

The Philippine Assembly was the ancestor of today's House of Representatives. Its first session is remembered as the beginning of the Philippine Congress, and Osmeña is honored as its first speaker.[3]

The Grand Opera House where it met no longer stands. But the idea it represented, Filipinos choosing their own representatives to make their own laws, became one of the foundations of the independent republic that followed in 1946.[1]
