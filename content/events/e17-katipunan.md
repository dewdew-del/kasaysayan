## The Liga lasts four days

In June 1892, José Rizal came home to Manila. On 3 July he met a group of reformers at a house on Ilaya Street in Tondo and founded La Liga Filipina, an organization for peaceful reform and mutual help among Filipinos.[2]

Four days later, Spanish authorities arrested him. Pamphlets criticizing the friars had been found in his sister's luggage, and the governor-general ordered him deported to Dapitan, a remote town in northern Mindanao.[2]

For some of the men who had joined the Liga, Rizal's arrest settled an argument. Peaceful reform, they decided, would never work.[1]

## A meeting on Azcárraga Street

On the night of 7 July 1892, the same day Rizal's deportation was announced, a small group met in a house on Calle Azcárraga, near the border of Tondo and San Nicolás. Among them were Andrés Bonifacio, Ladislao Diwa, Teodoro Plata, Deodato Arellano, Valentín Díaz and José Dizon.[1][3]

They founded a secret society. Its full name was Kataastaasan, Kagalanggalangang Katipunan ng mga Anak ng Bayan, "the Highest and Most Respected Society of the Children of the Nation." It was known simply as the Katipunan, or KKK. Its goal was independence from Spain, won by force if necessary. Its members swore to defend the poor and the oppressed and to treat each other as brothers.[1]

Some historians note that newly found documents suggest the group may have begun forming even earlier in 1892, but 7 July is the date traditionally given.[1]

## Andrés Bonifacio

Bonifacio was 28 years old. Born in Tondo in 1863, he had been orphaned young and supported his younger siblings by making canes and paper fans, then by working as a clerk and agent for trading firms. He had little formal schooling but read widely, including Rizal's novels, books about the French Revolution and the lives of the presidents of the United States.[1][3]

He was not the first president of the Katipunan, but he soon became its leader and its guiding spirit, known as the Supremo.[1]

## Why a secret society

Open political organizing was impossible under Spanish rule. Newspapers were censored, meetings watched and critics arrested or exiled, as Rizal's fate had just shown. A secret society, modeled partly on Freemasonry, which several founders knew, was a way to organize without being caught.[1]

The Katipunan also offered something the reform movement had not: a place for ordinary working people. Its early members were clerks, laborers, artisans and small traders, not wealthy ilustrados educated in Europe.[1][3]

## Blood oaths and code names

New members joined in a frightening ceremony. Blindfolded, they were led into a dark room, questioned, and finally signed their oath in their own blood, drawn from a cut on the arm. They took code names and learned passwords and secret signs.[1]

The society was organized in cells: a supreme council, provincial councils and local chapters, so that members knew only a few others. Its writings, including the Kartilya by Emilio Jacinto, taught a code of honesty, equality and love of country.[1][4]

## Growing in secret

Over the next four years, the Katipunan grew from a handful of men into a movement with thousands of members in Manila and the surrounding provinces, especially Cavite, Bulacan, Laguna and Batangas. Women joined too, in a separate section, among them Bonifacio's wife Gregoria de Jesús.[1][4]

The society printed a newspaper, Kalayaan ("Freedom"), in 1896, and members collected money and gathered weapons, though they never had nearly enough guns.[1]

## Toward revolution

In August 1896, the Katipunan was discovered by the Spanish, and its members were forced to begin the revolution sooner than planned.[1] The secret society founded on Azcárraga Street became the army of the Philippine Revolution, the first revolution in Asia against a Western colonial power.

The site of the founding is marked today on Claro M. Recto Avenue in Manila, the modern name of the old street.[3]
