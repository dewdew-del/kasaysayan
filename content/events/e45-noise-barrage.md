## An election under martial law

Six years after declaring martial law, President Ferdinand Marcos announced that the country would elect an Interim Batasang Pambansa, a legislature that would sit alongside him. The election was set for 7 April 1978.[1]

For the government, it was a way to show the world that the Philippines still had democracy. For the opposition, it was a hard choice: take part in an election run by the regime, or boycott it.[1][2]

{{photo:2}}

## Ninoy runs from prison

Senator Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr. had been in prison since the night martial law was declared in 1972. In 1977 a military commission had sentenced him to death by firing squad, though the sentence was not carried out.[1]

In 1978, Aquino decided to run from his cell. He formed a new party, Lakas ng Bayan, "Strength of the Nation," known by its acronym LABAN, which also means "fight." It fielded 21 candidates for the Metro Manila seats, led by Aquino himself.[1][3]

Aquino was not allowed to campaign in person. He appeared once on a television interview program, speaking from detention, and his frankness surprised many viewers. His family and supporters campaigned for him.[1]

## The night of noise

The opposition could not hold big rallies or count on the newspapers. So it asked people to protest in a way the government could not easily stop.[1]

On the night of 6 April 1978, the eve of the election, LABAN called on Metro Manila residents to make noise to show their support. At an agreed time, people began banging pots and pans, honking car horns, blowing whistles and ringing church bells. Jeepneys and cars drove through the streets with horns blaring. Some people set off firecrackers.[1][3]

The noise went on for hours, in some neighborhoods until dawn. Accounts describe it as one of the biggest acts of protest since martial law began.[1][3]

## A safe way to say no

The noise barrage was clever because it was hard to punish. Open dissent could get people arrested. But noise could come from anywhere, from any house or car in the dark, and no one could be singled out.[1]

For many people who had been afraid to speak for six years, it was the first time they had joined a protest. They discovered that many of their neighbors felt the same way.[2]

## What people remembered

Many people who took part remembered the night for the rest of their lives. They recalled families standing at their windows banging kitchen pans, neighbors they had never spoken to joining in, and the strange feeling of hearing a whole city make noise together after years of silence.[1][2]

The government's media did not report the protest. But everyone in Metro Manila had heard it, and word of it spread to the provinces by letters, phone calls and travelers.[1]

## The result

The official results gave all 21 Metro Manila seats to the administration party, Kilusang Bagong Lipunan, whose list was led by First Lady Imelda Marcos. All the LABAN candidates, including Aquino, lost.[1][3]

The opposition said the election was rigged. Aquino later said the noise barrage had been so loud that it convinced Marcos to make sure no LABAN candidate won.[3] After the election, some opposition supporters who protested the results were arrested.[1]

## What it led to

In 1980, Aquino was allowed to go to the United States for heart surgery. He stayed in exile for three years.[1]

The noise barrage of 1978 showed that ordinary people could still find ways to resist. Its name lived on: an armed opposition group formed in 1980 called itself the April 6 Liberation Movement. More importantly, the shared experience of making noise together prepared many people for the much larger protests that came after Aquino's assassination in 1983, and for EDSA in 1986.[1][2]
