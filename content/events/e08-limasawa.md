## After the Pacific

In March 1521, the three remaining ships of Ferdinand Magellan's expedition came out of the Pacific Ocean after a crossing that had lasted more than three months. Many sailors had died of hunger and scurvy. On 16 March they sighted land, and soon after anchored at Homonhon, a small island off Samar, to rest and find water.[2]

A few days later they sailed on, and came to an island the expedition's chronicler, the Italian Antonio Pigafetta, called Mazaua.[1]

## Two rulers and a blood compact

At Mazaua, Pigafetta wrote, the Europeans met a local ruler, Rajah Kolambu, and his relative Rajah Siagu from a nearby land. Gifts were exchanged. Magellan and Kolambu made a blood compact, a ritual of friendship in which each drank a little of the other's blood mixed with wine.[2]

Pigafetta described the people's gold ornaments, their boats and their food in detail. He was one of very few people on the voyage who kept a careful diary, and much of what we know about this first meeting comes from him.[1][2]

## Easter Sunday

On 31 March 1521, which was Easter Sunday, the expedition's chaplain celebrated Mass on the island. Pigafetta wrote that the two rulers attended. After the Mass, a large wooden cross was raised on the highest hill as a sign that the land had been claimed for the Spanish king.[1][2]

The Mass at Mazaua is remembered as the first Catholic Mass in the Philippines, the beginning of a religion that today is practised by most Filipinos. In 2021, the Philippines marked its 500th anniversary.[3]

## Food, gold and a cross

Pigafetta was fascinated by what he saw. He wrote about the rulers' gold earrings and gold-decorated daggers, the painted bodies of the men, the rice and pork they were served, and the palm wine they drank from porcelain cups. To starving sailors who had just crossed the Pacific, the welcome must have seemed almost unreal.[2]

He also noted the meaning of the cross raised on the hill. Magellan told the rulers that it would protect them, and that if other Europeans came they should show it as a sign of friendship. For the Spanish, it was also a claim: the cross marked the land as theirs in the eyes of their king.[1][2]

## Which island was Mazaua?

Here the story becomes a puzzle. Pigafetta's word "Mazaua" does not appear on modern maps. For centuries the island was identified as Limasawa, a small island off the southern tip of Leyte, in what is now Southern Leyte province.[1]

In the 20th century some historians challenged this. They argued that Mazaua was really Masao, at the mouth of the Agusan River in Butuan, in northern Mindanao. The historian Sonia Zaide was among the best-known supporters of the Butuan view, citing details from Pigafetta's diary.[1][2]

In 1995 a congresswoman from Butuan filed a bill contesting the Limasawa claim, and Congress asked the National Historical Institute to study the question.[1]

## Three panels, one answer

The government's historical body has looked at the question three times. A first panel, led by former Supreme Court Justice Emilio Gancayco, reported in the late 1990s. A second, led by historian Benito Legarda, followed in 2008. Both favored Limasawa.[1][2]

In 2018 the National Historical Commission of the Philippines formed a third panel, chaired by historian Resil Mojares. After a year of reviewing the evidence and the arguments of the Butuan side, it concluded that they were "not sufficient and convincing enough" to reverse the ruling. The commission adopted that report in 2020.[1]

A key point in the panels' reasoning is geography. Pigafetta describes Mazaua as an island lying off the southwestern tip of Leyte. Limasawa fits that description. Masao in Butuan is not an island but a place on the Mindanao coast.[1]

## A debate that stays alive

Supporters of Butuan have not given up, and the debate still appears in classrooms and newspapers.[2] This page follows the commission's ruling in placing the Mass at Limasawa, while recording that the question was argued for decades.

It is also a good example of how history works. A single eyewitness account, written five hundred years ago in Italian, with place names spelled by ear, can still be read in different ways, and careful people can disagree about what it means.
