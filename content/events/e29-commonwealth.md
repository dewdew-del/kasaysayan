## A promise with a date

For three decades after the Philippine Assembly opened, Filipino leaders lobbied Washington for independence. In 1934 they finally won a law with a date. The Tydings–McDuffie Act, passed by the US Congress, promised full independence after a ten-year transition under a Filipino-run government called the Commonwealth.[1][3]

American motives were mixed. Some Americans supported Filipino independence on principle. Others, especially farmers and labor groups during the Great Depression, wanted to stop tax-free imports of Philippine sugar and the arrival of Filipino workers.[3]

## A constitution

A constitutional convention met in Manila from July 1934. Its delegates wrote a constitution with a president, a single-chamber National Assembly and a bill of rights, modeled partly on the American system. It was approved by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in March 1935 and by Filipino voters in a plebiscite in May.[1][2]

## The first election

In September 1935, Filipinos elected their first president. Manuel L. Quezon, who had been Senate president for nearly twenty years, ran with Sergio Osmeña as his running mate. They easily defeated Emilio Aguinaldo, the old revolutionary general, and Bishop Gregorio Aglipay, founder of the Philippine Independent Church.[3]

Quezon wrote in his autobiography that he saw his election as the fulfillment of a lifetime's work.[3]

## Fifteenth of November

On the morning of 15 November 1935, a huge crowd, estimated at about 300,000 people, gathered in front of the Legislative Building in Manila. American officials, Filipino leaders, foreign guests and ordinary people from across the islands filled the streets.[1][4]

The US secretary of war, George Dern, read President Roosevelt's proclamation establishing the Commonwealth. Then Quezon took his oath as president and Osmeña as vice president. The American governor-general's office was replaced by a high commissioner, and for the first time a Filipino was the head of the government of the islands.[1][2]

## A new kind of government

Under the Commonwealth, Filipinos ran almost all of their own domestic affairs for the first time. Filipino officials headed the departments of government, the courts, the schools and the provinces. The National Assembly, elected by Filipino voters, made the laws.[2]

The inauguration was also a moment of national celebration. Newspapers printed special editions, towns held parades, and Filipinos who had grown up under foreign rule saw one of their own sworn in as head of the government.[1][4]

## Building a nation

The new government moved quickly on several fronts. It passed a National Defense Act to build a Philippine army, with General Douglas MacArthur as military adviser. It set up the Institute of National Language, which in 1937 chose Tagalog as the basis for a national language. It held a plebiscite in 1937 in which women won the right to vote.[1][3]

Quezon spoke often of "social justice." The Commonwealth began programs to buy up large estates and distribute land, and to improve conditions for workers. But poverty, tenant unrest and the power of large landowners remained serious problems.[3]

## Independence, almost

The Commonwealth had its own flag, flown alongside the American flag, and its own anthem. Filipinos could travel, study and work in the United States more freely than later, but they were classed as "nationals," not citizens.[3]


The Commonwealth was a real step toward independence, but not the whole step. The United States still controlled foreign affairs and defense, and could overrule Philippine laws in some areas. Trade remained tied to the American market.[3]

## Cut short by war

The ten years were never completed in peace. In December 1941 Japan invaded the Philippines. In 1942 Quezon left for the United States, where he led a government in exile in Washington. He died in New York in August 1944, before the islands were freed, and Osmeña succeeded him.[3]

Independence came on 4 July 1946, a little more than ten years after that November morning in Manila. The Legislative Building where the Commonwealth was inaugurated, badly damaged in 1945 and later rebuilt, is now part of the National Museum.[1]
