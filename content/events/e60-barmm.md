## A long war

The Moro peoples of Mindanao, Sulu and Palawan, the Muslim communities of the south, have a history older than the Philippine state. Their sultanates resisted Spain for centuries. Under American and then Philippine rule, many Moros felt that their land, faith and identity were being taken from them, as settlers from the north moved into Mindanao.[1][3]

In the early 1970s, an armed rebellion began, led by the Moro National Liberation Front. Tens of thousands of people were killed over the following decades, and many more were displaced.[1]

## Peace attempts

The government and the MNLF signed peace agreements in 1976 and 1996. In 1989 an Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao, the ARMM, was created. But many Moros felt it had little real power and money, and it was troubled by poverty and corruption.[1][3]

Another group, the Moro Islamic Liberation Front, MILF, which had split from the MNLF, kept fighting. Talks with it went on, broke down and resumed many times over almost two decades.[1]

## The 2014 agreement

In March 2014, the government of President Benigno Aquino III and the MILF signed the Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro. It promised to replace the ARMM with a new autonomous region with more powers and a larger share of resources.[1][3]

The law needed to make it real stalled in Congress after a police operation in Mamasapano, Maguindanao, in January 2015 ended in a clash that killed 44 police commandos, along with MILF fighters and civilians. Public anger made passage impossible for a time.[1]

## The Bangsamoro Organic Law

Under President Rodrigo Duterte, Congress finally passed the Bangsamoro Organic Law, signed in July 2018. It created the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao, the BARMM, with its own parliament, a ministerial form of government, a block grant from the national budget, and greater control over local resources and justice, including Sharia courts for Muslims.[3]

## The vote

The law had to be approved by the people in a plebiscite. The first round was held on 21 January 2019 in the ARMM provinces and in Cotabato City and Isabela City. A second round on 6 February decided whether nearby towns and villages would join.[2][3]

Most voters said yes. On 25 January, the election commission declared the law ratified. Cotabato City voted to join the new region. In Sulu, voters rejected it, and the Supreme Court later ruled that the province was not part of the BARMM.[2][3]

## Why people voted yes

Many voters said they were tired of war. Families who had lived through decades of fighting, displacement and poverty hoped that real self-government would bring peace, investment and jobs.[1]

Others were cautious. Some local leaders and Christian communities in the area worried about how the new region would treat them, and some Muslim voters doubted that the new government would be better than the old ARMM. The campaign was intense, but the vote was largely peaceful.[1][3]

## A new government

On 22 February 2019, the Bangsamoro Transition Authority, an appointed interim parliament led by MILF members, was sworn in. MILF chairman Murad Ebrahim, once a rebel commander, became the region's interim chief minister. The ARMM was formally turned over to the new region on 26 February.[1][3]

Thousands of MILF fighters began to lay down their weapons in stages, in exchange for help returning to civilian life.[1]

## A work in progress

The transition has not been easy. The first elections for the Bangsamoro parliament were postponed more than once. Some armed groups remain outside the peace process, and the region is still among the poorest in the country.[4]

Still, for many in the region, the BARMM is the closest the Moro peoples have come to self-government within the Philippines. Supporters called the 2019 vote a victory for people tired of war.[1] Its success or failure will shape the future of Mindanao for decades.
