## Four years in Dapitan

After his arrest in July 1892, José Rizal spent four years in exile in Dapitan, a small town on the northern coast of Mindanao. He made the best of it. He practised medicine, treating patients for free or for small fees, ran a school for local boys, built a water system for the town, collected plants and animals for European scientists, and fell in love with Josephine Bracken, an Irish woman who had come to Dapitan with a patient.[3]

When the Katipunan was founded, its members admired Rizal and used his name as a password. In 1896 they sent Pío Valenzuela to Dapitan to ask his support for a revolution. Rizal refused, saying the people were not ready and had too few weapons.[3]

## A volunteer doctor

Rizal had volunteered to serve as an army doctor in Cuba, where Spain was fighting a rebellion, and in 1896 his request was granted. He was on his way to Spain by ship when the revolution broke out in August.[1][3]

During the voyage, the Spanish authorities decided he was a danger after all. He was arrested on board and sent back to Manila, where he was imprisoned at Fort Santiago in November 1896.[3]

## The trial

On 26 December 1896, Rizal was tried by a military court in Manila. He was accused of rebellion, sedition and forming illegal associations, and of being the brains behind the revolution.[1][3]

The trial was quick, and its outcome was never really in doubt. Rizal's defense lawyer, a young Spanish army officer named Luis Taviel de Andrade, argued well, but the court sentenced Rizal to death. The governor-general, Camilo de Polavieja, confirmed the sentence on 28 December and set the execution for seven o'clock on the morning of 30 December.[3]

## The last night

On the night of 29 December, in his cell at Fort Santiago, Rizal wrote a long poem in Spanish, a farewell to his country. It had no title. When his family visited, he told his sister Trinidad in English, "There is something inside," and gave her a small alcohol stove. The poem was hidden in it.[2][3]

The poem, later titled Mi último adiós ("My Last Farewell"), begins with a farewell to the "adored Fatherland." In it, Rizal tells his country he gives his life gladly, and asks to be remembered.[2][4] It became one of the most famous poems in Philippine history.

## Bagumbayan

Early on the morning of 30 December 1896, Rizal walked from Fort Santiago to the field of Bagumbayan, outside the walls of Manila, where the three priests of Gomburza had died in 1872. Soldiers lined the route, and a crowd gathered.[1]

He asked to be shot facing the firing squad, but the officer refused: traitors were shot in the back. As the shots rang out at about 7:03 a.m., Rizal is said to have twisted his body so that he fell facing the sky. His last words were reported to be "Consummatum est," "It is finished."[1][3]

He was 35 years old.

## Josephine and the family

Rizal's family were allowed to see him on his last day. His mother, Teodora Alonso, and his sisters came to Fort Santiago. Josephine Bracken also visited. Some accounts say that Rizal and Josephine were married by a priest in the early hours before his execution, after Rizal signed a retraction of his criticisms of the Church. Whether the marriage and the retraction really happened has been debated by historians for more than a century.[3]

## A martyr for the revolution

The Spanish hoped that executing Rizal would frighten the revolutionaries. It did the opposite. His death turned the man who had preached reform into a martyr, and many Filipinos who had hesitated now joined the fight.[1]

The field where he died is now Rizal Park, also called the Luneta, and a monument stands over his remains. Every 30 December, Rizal Day, the country remembers him as its national hero.[1][3]
