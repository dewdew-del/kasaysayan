## After the revolution

When Corazon Aquino became president in February 1986, the country was still governed by the 1973 Constitution written under martial law. She set it aside. In March 1986 she issued Proclamation No. 3, a temporary "Freedom Constitution" that let her govern while a new charter was written.[1][2]

The new president had enormous power for a time: she could issue laws, replace officials and reorganize the government. She promised to give that power up as soon as a new constitution was in place.[2]

## The Constitutional Commission

In May 1986, Aquino appointed a Constitutional Commission of 48 members to draft the new charter. They included lawyers, judges, former senators, church people, labor and farmers' leaders, business people and a few supporters of the old regime. It was chaired by Cecilia Muñoz-Palma, a former Supreme Court justice and the first woman to serve on that court.[1][2]

The commission met for four months at the old Batasang Pambansa building in Quezon City. Its debates were broadcast and reported widely. Members argued over the shape of the government, the US military bases, land reform, the death penalty, the rights of Muslims and indigenous peoples, and much more.[1]

## Never again

The writers of the 1987 Constitution had one experience on their minds: the fourteen years of Marcos's rule. Many provisions were designed so that no president could do what he had done.[2]

The president would serve only one six-year term, with no re-election. Martial law could be declared only for 60 days at a time, and Congress could revoke it. The Supreme Court could review whether there was a real basis for it. Congress could not be closed, and the courts would keep working.[2][3]

A long Bill of Rights protected freedom of speech, of the press and of assembly, and banned torture, secret detention and incommunicado arrest. A new Commission on Human Rights was created to investigate abuses.[2]

## Other big choices

The constitution returned the country to a presidential system with a two-chamber Congress, a Senate and a House of Representatives. It created autonomous regions for Muslim Mindanao and the Cordillera, promised land reform, and set aside seats in the House for sectors such as labor, farmers, women and indigenous peoples.[1][2]

It declared Filipino the national language and said English would remain an official language. It limited foreign ownership of land and many businesses. And it said that after 1991, foreign military bases could stay only under a treaty approved by the Senate, a provision that would matter very soon.[2]

The commission finished its draft on 12 October 1986.[1]

## The vote

On 2 February 1987, Filipinos voted in a nationwide plebiscite, by secret ballot. It was a very different scene from the citizens' assemblies and shows of hands of 1973.[1][3]

Turnout was very high. About three in four voters, roughly 76 to 77 percent, approved the new constitution. On 11 February 1987, President Aquino proclaimed it ratified.[1][2]

## The campaign

Supporters of the new constitution campaigned for a "yes" vote as a way to complete the work of EDSA and give President Aquino's government a firm legal foundation. Church leaders and many civic groups backed it.[1]

Opponents came from different directions. Marcos loyalists rejected it outright. Some on the left argued that it did not go far enough on land reform and on the US bases. Others objected to particular provisions, such as those on the family, the economy or the military.[1][2]

## Still in force

The 1987 Constitution is the charter the Philippines lives under today. It has never been amended, though presidents and congresses have proposed changes many times, often called "Charter change" or "Cha-cha." Each proposal has met strong opposition, partly because of fears that politicians would use it to lift term limits.[1]

For many Filipinos, the constitution is EDSA written down: a promise, made after a dictatorship, that power would be limited, rights protected and martial law never abused again.[2]
