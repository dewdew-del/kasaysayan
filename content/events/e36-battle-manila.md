## Racing to Manila

In January 1945, American forces landed at Lingayen Gulf and pushed south across the central plain of Luzon toward Manila. General Douglas MacArthur wanted the capital taken quickly. Thousands of American and other Allied civilians were held in prison camps in the city, and many were starving.[4]

On the night of 3 February 1945, an American flying column reached the University of Santo Tomas, where some 3,700 internees were held, and freed them.[1][4] Over the next days, American troops entered the northern part of the city.

{{photo:2}}

## A city that would not be spared

The Japanese army commander in the Philippines, General Tomoyuki Yamashita, had planned to withdraw his main forces into the mountains rather than fight for Manila. But about 16,000 Japanese troops, mostly naval forces under Rear Admiral Sanji Iwabuchi, stayed and decided to fight to the death.[1][2]

They blew up bridges over the Pasig River, burned buildings north of the river and turned the southern districts, Intramuros, Ermita, Malate and Paco, into fortresses.[1]

## Street by street

What followed was some of the worst urban fighting of the Second World War. American and Filipino troops, among them guerrillas who knew the city, fought block by block and building by building. To avoid losing soldiers, American commanders used heavy artillery against buildings where Japanese troops were holding out, many of them full of civilians.[2][4]

Some of the grandest buildings of the city became battlefields: the Manila Hotel, the Legislative Building, the post office, the city hall, the old churches and universities.[1]

{{photo:3}}

## The massacres

As they were trapped and losing, Japanese troops turned on the civilians around them. In homes, churches, schools, hospitals and clubs across Ermita, Malate and Intramuros, men, women and children were shot, bayoneted, burned and raped.[1][2]

Hundreds were killed at the German Club, in La Salle College, in churches where people had taken shelter, and in the streets. Survivors' testimony later formed part of the war crimes trials after the war.[2]

## Intramuros falls

The last Japanese stronghold was Intramuros, the old walled city. American artillery pounded its walls for days. When troops finally broke in, in late February, they found churches, convents, schools and houses reduced to rubble, and many civilians dead. Of the old churches inside the walls, only San Agustin survived.[1][3]

The battle officially ended on 3 March 1945.[1]

## Life amid the ruins

For the people who survived, the battle meant weeks of hiding in basements, air-raid shelters and church crypts, often with little food or water, while shells fell outside. Families were separated, and many never learned exactly how their relatives had died.[1][4]

When the fighting stopped, survivors walked through streets of rubble looking for missing family members and for anything left of their homes. Refugees crowded the northern districts that had been taken first. Rebuilding would take years.[4]

## The cost

At least 100,000 civilians are believed to have died in the month of fighting, in the massacres and from the shelling, out of a city of about a million people. Some estimates are higher. How many were killed by the Japanese and how many by American artillery is still debated; one estimate suggests about a third died from shelling.[3][4]

Manila was one of the most heavily destroyed cities of the war. Much of what had made it the "Pearl of the Orient," its old churches, Spanish-era houses, libraries and archives, was gone.[2]

## Memorare

For decades, the battle was overshadowed by the story of liberation. In 1995, on the fiftieth anniversary, survivors unveiled a monument in Intramuros, the Memorare Manila 1945, a sculpture of a woman holding a dead child, surrounded by grieving figures. Its inscription remembers the "more than 100,000 defenseless civilians" who died.[3]

Each February, survivors and their families gather there to remember.[3] For them, 1945 is not only the year Manila was freed, but the year it was lost.
